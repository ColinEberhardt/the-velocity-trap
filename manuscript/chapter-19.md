# Chapter 19 — The Thing Worth Grieving

*Draft — first pass.*

---

Sam had heard, somehow — the building always found a way to move its worst news faster than its best — that his name had come up in a boardroom, and it had done something to him that four days of investigation hadn't managed until now.

He wasn't at his desk. Maya found him in the stairwell, the one nobody used because the lift was faster, sitting on the top step with his knees drawn up like someone considerably younger than twenty-three.

"I heard someone wanted to fire me," he said, before she'd sat down. "In the board meeting. Someone actually said my name."

"Someone suggested it. It didn't happen, and it isn't going to, and I said so myself, in the room, to eleven people who needed to hear it." She sat on the step below him, because it put her at his eye level rather than above it, which she'd noticed mattered more than people expected. "I need you to hear the next part properly, Sam, not just the part where you're safe. You didn't cause this. Not because I'm being kind. Because I've spent every day since I got here finding the actual shape of it, and your name is the smallest, least interesting part of that shape."

"But I still feel like it's mine." His voice cracked slightly on *mine*, and he pressed on before she could answer, the way people do when stopping might mean losing the nerve to say the rest. "Everyone keeps telling me it's systemic, it's the org, it's the tooling, and I believe all of that, I do — but none of it changes the fact that I was the one who pressed the button. I was the one who didn't ask enough questions. A man almost died because of code I shipped, and I keep coming back to how ordinary it felt at the time — I didn't feel like I was making a decision that mattered that much, and it did, and I think that's the part I can't get past. If I'd been better at this job, none of it happens."

"If you'd had fifteen years of scar tissue instead of four months, maybe none of it happens," Maya said. "That's not a fact about you being insufficient, Sam. It's a fact about how much time it actually takes to build the thing you were missing, and nobody in this building gave you the fifteen years, or even told you that you needed them, because everyone around you was pretending the tools had made that requirement obsolete."

Marcus arrived without either of them noticing, and sat on the step below both of them, which meant he ended up lowest in the stairwell, looking up, and something about the geometry of it seemed deliberate.

"Can I tell you something that isn't reassurance," he said, "because I think you've had enough reassurance today and none of it's landed."

Sam nodded, wary.

"What you're feeling isn't a symptom to be managed until it goes away. It's grief, and it's a completely appropriate response to a real loss. You trained for years to become something, and the something you trained for changed shape while you were mid-flight, and nobody sent you a memo, and you're allowed to be sad about that the same way you'd be allowed to be sad about any other real thing that ended before you were ready. The people telling you to just get over it and enjoy how fast everything is now are not more resilient than you. They've just had longer to stop saying the sad part out loud. That's not the same as not feeling it." He said it without softness exactly, but without any distance either — the tone of a man reporting something he'd verified rather than something he hoped was true. "Josh feels a version of it. I'd put money on half this building feeling some version of it. You're not broken for noticing first. You're just the one who hadn't yet learned it wasn't allowed to be said."

Sam's eyes had gone bright, and he didn't try to hide it, which Maya took as its own kind of progress. "Then what do I do with it?"

"You don't do anything with it today. Today you let it be true without needing to solve it." Marcus stood, knees audibly protesting the stairwell. "Eventually — not today — you'll find out whether you still love the parts of this work that are actually yours. The framing of a problem. The judgement about what matters. The thing you still do for fun in the evenings, that you mentioned to me like it was an embarrassing secret, when it's the most useful sentence you've said all week. That's not nothing, Sam. That might turn out to be everything that's actually left, and it might turn out to be enough. But you don't have to know that yet. You're allowed to just be sad on a Tuesday, on some stairs, for a job that stopped existing without asking your permission."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. Maya found, sitting on a cold concrete step in a stairwell nobody used, that she didn't especially want to be anywhere else.
