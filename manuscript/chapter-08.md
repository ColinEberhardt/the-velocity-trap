# Chapter 8 — Shun

*Draft — first pass.*

---

It was eight seats, a counter, and nothing on the walls that looked like it had been chosen by a designer, which Maya clocked immediately as its own kind of expensive.

"Marcus." A woman came out from behind the counter wiping her hands on an apron that had seen real use, not display use, and said his name the way you say a word you're pleased to still have a use for. "You didn't call ahead."

"I never call ahead. You always have room."

"I always *make* room, which is different, and you know it's different, and you do it to me anyway." She looked at Maya with open, unhurried curiosity — not the polite scan of a host sizing up a stranger, but something closer to actual interest. "This is new."

"Maya Reyes. She's the reason I'm not fishing this month."

"Aiko Sato." She didn't offer a hand — busy hands, Maya noted, flour or something like it worked into the creases — but nodded, once, the way you'd acknowledge someone whose measure you intended to take slowly, over the course of an evening, rather than all at once. "Sit. I'll feed you both before anyone says anything useful. Useful things go better on a full stomach and I don't want you thinking on my counter with an empty one, it's bad for my reputation."

Maya sat. It was, against every instinct she owned, a relief to be told what to do by somebody who clearly meant it kindly.

They ate in near-silence for the first twenty minutes — small, precise plates, arriving with no menu and no explanation, each one finished before the next appeared — and Maya spent most of it watching the kitchen rather than the food, because there was only one person in it, one line cook, and the two of them worked in a rhythm so tight it looked almost slow, the way a well-thrown ball looks slow to the person who's about to catch it and knows exactly where to put their hands.

"You're not going to tell me why we're here," she said eventually.

"I'm not," Marcus agreed. "You'll work it out or you won't. I've stopped explaining things people are capable of finding for themselves — it's a worse use of both our time than it looks."

Aiko reappeared between courses, unbidden, and refilled nothing, corrected nothing, simply stood for a moment with her arms folded, watching her own kitchen the way Maya imagined a conductor might watch an orchestra she'd already stopped needing to conduct.

"How many people work here," Maya asked her.

"Two. Me and Yuki." A tilt of the head toward the young man at the pass, who didn't look up, entirely absorbed in something involving fish and a blowtorch. "For now, just Yuki. He's been with me two years. Next year, if he's ready, he touches the tuna."

"Two years before he touches the tuna."

"Two years before he touches *my* tuna, on *my* counter, in front of *my* guests. He can do a great deal else. He's been doing a great deal else since week one — prep, sauces, service, everything that teaches him what the ingredient actually is before I let him be the last hand it passes through." Something in Aiko's face suggested this was not a rule she enjoyed defending, only one she'd stopped apologising for. "People think it's about tradition. It's not sentimental. It's that the tuna doesn't forgive a mistake made by somebody who hasn't yet built the judgement to know they're making one."

"That sounds," Maya said, careful with the word, "a little like something out of a fortune cookie."

Marcus, for the first time all evening, looked faintly pleased with her.

"It would be," Aiko said, entirely unbothered, "if I couldn't tell you exactly what it cost me to learn it. Twelve years ago I ran four restaurants. Forty covers a night across all of them, more on weekends, a brigade of thirty-one people whose names I'd stopped being able to guarantee I knew. I built it fast because I was good and because everyone kept telling me I should, and it made money, and it was, by every measure anyone was applying to me at the time, a success." She wiped the counter in front of Maya, though there was nothing on it. "Then a boy who'd been with me four months plated a dish with the wrong garnish on it. Cross-contamination. A guest with a shellfish allergy went to hospital. He survived. I closed three of the four restaurants within the year, because the truth I couldn't get away from was that I no longer knew, in any of them, whose judgement I was actually trusting when a plate left the kitchen. I'd built something too big for me to vouch for."

The kitchen had gone quiet enough that Maya could hear the specific hiss of the blowtorch cutting out.

"So this is the whole business now. One counter. Eight seats. Two people, both of whom I can vouch for personally, because I watched both of them build the judgement they're using tonight, one boring service at a time." She looked at Marcus, and something passed between them that had clearly been said before, in some other year, in some other kitchen. "He knows this story. I don't know why he's made you sit through it, but I imagine he has a reason, and I imagine you'll like it less than the fish."

"I usually do," Maya said.

Marcus said nothing at all, which she was beginning to understand was, with him, its own kind of answer.
