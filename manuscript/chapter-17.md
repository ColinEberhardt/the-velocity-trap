# Chapter 17 — What Yuki Isn't Allowed to Skip

*Draft — first pass. Second Shun visit — must land both the "small, empowered team" connection and the sharper judgement-building point (see `writing-plan.md` §4a/§4b) explicitly, not just atmospherically.*

---

Four days left, and Marcus had still insisted on the booking, which by now Maya had stopped fighting him on, because she'd noticed something over the last week: she thought more clearly after an hour at this counter than after any hour spent staring at a dependency graph, and she was tired enough not to argue with evidence just because it inconvenienced her.

Yuki was slicing something pale and precise, fast, unhesitating — visibly more confident than the first time she'd watched him, nine days and a lifetime of investigation ago.

"He's better," Maya said.

"He's had two more years of evenings like this one since the last time you were paying attention to him," Aiko said, without looking up from the pass, which was its own kind of joke, since it had been nine days, not two years, and both of them knew she meant something else by it. "You're doing the thing people do. Watching the speed and mistaking it for the whole skill."

"What's the whole skill, if it isn't that?"

"Knowing when to stop." Aiko nodded at a small stack of trimmings Yuki had set carefully to one side rather than discarded. "Anyone can learn to cut fast. I could teach a machine to cut faster than him by Thursday. What he's actually spent two years building is the part that doesn't show in the cut at all — knowing which piece isn't good enough before it reaches a plate, and being willing to lose it rather than serve it. That's not in his hands. That's a decision, made a hundred times a service, and the only way anyone's ever learned to make it well is by making it badly first, a lot, somewhere nobody was watching closely enough to make the mistake expensive."

Maya thought of Sam, in the small glass room, saying *I know that it's wrong, I don't think I understand it well enough to know I should've been scared of it* — and felt something click into a shape she'd been circling for days without quite landing on.

"He read the code," she said, half to herself.

Marcus looked up.

"Sam. He told me he read every line — the bits he wrote and the bits the AI wrote for him. He wasn't lazy. He wasn't careless. He genuinely reviewed it, carefully, the way we keep telling people to review things." She said it slower now, testing the shape of it as it came out. "And it didn't matter. Reading it carefully gave him none of the thing Yuki's actually spent two years building. You can watch someone else make the right call a thousand times and still not have the judgement to make it yourself, because judgement isn't something you absorb by looking at the outcome. It's built by being the one who got it wrong and had to live with knowing why."

"Yes," Marcus said, simply, in the tone of a man confirming an answer rather than teaching one.

"So it's not that AI lets people skip the boring years." She was talking to herself now more than either of them, the way she did when something was actually landing rather than just being said. "It's that even doing the responsible thing — reading carefully, reviewing properly — doesn't get you the years back. There isn't a shortcut through judgement. There's only the slow way, or not having it."

"That's the part everyone gets wrong when they hear this story," Aiko said, wiping the counter, unhurried. "They think the answer is: slow the machine down. Make the AI ask more questions, be more careful, take longer. That's not nothing, but it isn't the actual fix. The actual fix is deciding, on purpose, that some of your people's time is going to be spent building judgement that doesn't show up as output this quarter — protecting that on purpose, the way I protect Yuki's first two years, even though a machine could plate faster than him by lunchtime tomorrow. Nobody does that by accident anymore. It has to be a decision somebody keeps making, over and over, against every incentive telling them to stop."

Maya thought about the dashboard Felix loved, green all the way across, and the four-minute review, and the skill that had been trimmed for conciseness eight times by six people who'd each made a small, individually reasonable choice to go slightly faster. Nobody had decided to remove judgement from the building. They had simply never decided, on purpose, to protect it, and it had bled out through eight small doors nobody had thought to close.

"There's your team, too," she said, looking at Marcus now, not Aiko. "Two people. Everything either of them does, the other one can see the whole shape of. Yuki's not on a rotation across four kitchens he's never been in. He's not reviewing a stranger's pull request in four minutes between two other things. He's here, every night, watching the one person whose judgement he's trying to build, in a space small enough that nothing either of them does is invisible to the other." She felt the shape of it complete itself, the way a held breath finally goes out. "That's what Tariq's graph was actually showing me. Not that the code drifted. That nobody was close enough to anything, anymore, to notice when it did."

Marcus didn't say anything for a while, which she'd learned by now was the closest he came to applause.

"Write that down," he said eventually. "All of it. Exactly the way you just said it, before you lose the shape of it to something tidier. You're going to need it on Friday, and I don't think you'll say it this well twice."
