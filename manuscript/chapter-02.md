# Chapter 2 — Days, Not Months

*Draft — first pass. Lightly revised 2026-09-11 alongside the chapter 1 rewrite, so Kalu describes the incident accurately (a missed clinical alert in an internal system) rather than implying it happened inside an AI product.*

---

Maya was on her second coffee and her sister's voicemail — Sunday's call, three days late, the kind of thing she'd apologise for and then do again — when Priya, three years into the firm and sharper than she gave herself credit for, put her head round the door and said, "Kestrel's phone's ringing and it's not Kestrel's phone."

Kestrel's phone was the mobile that lived in the top drawer of Maya's desk, switched off except when it wasn't, and it only rang when someone had decided, somewhere above the level of a normal procurement process, that they had a problem they didn't want in writing yet.

She let it ring twice more before she picked it up, because answering on the first ring always made people trust you less.

"Maya Reyes."

"This is Robert Kalu, general counsel, Meridian Health Group. Are you able to talk."

Not a question, from the way he said it. She minimised the voicemail, minimised her sister's face frozen mid-sentence on the screen, and gave him her full attention, because *general counsel* three words into a call like this one meant somebody had already used the word *litigation* in a room that morning.

"I'm listening."

"We had a serious clinical safety incident ten days ago that we only fully understood last night. A critical blood result went unescalated for nine days. The patient's now in a coronary care unit. He's expected to recover. The system responsible is an old, unglamorous piece of internal software — nobody would look at it twice — and it was modified six weeks ago. The board is meeting again at eleven and wants an independent technical lead in the room who has no prior relationship with our digital function. Your name came up twice before I'd finished asking."

"Whose incident is it currently being called."

A pause. "I don't follow."

"Whose fault is it currently being called, Mr Kalu. Not whose fault it is. Whose fault it's *being called*, this morning, before anyone's actually looked."

Another pause, longer, and she heard him decide to be honest with her, which she took as a good sign about him and a bad sign about everyone else in that room. "There's an engineering team. There's a system that clearly failed. I imagine names will come up."

"I can be there by half eleven."

---

Meridian's head office was a glass building with a lot of daylight and not much air, the kind of place designed to photograph well for an annual report. Maya signed in as *M. Reyes, Kestrel Advisory* and was walked up eleven floors by a young man who introduced himself as someone's PA and then said nothing else, which told her everyone above the ground floor already knew more than they were supposed to say to a stranger in the lift.

The boardroom had the blinds down against a bright morning, which she also read as a decision rather than an accident. Six people at a long table, two more on a screen at the end. Deborah Ashworth sat at the head, and Maya knew her before anyone said her name, because she was the only person in the room whose face was doing exactly one thing — calm, contained, appropriately grave — while everyone else's was doing several things badly.

"Ms Reyes. Thank you for coming so quickly." Ashworth's voice had the particular evenness of someone who had rehearsed being calm long enough that it had stopped being a performance and become a skill. "I'll be direct, because I think that's what we're paying for. We have a patient in a coronary care unit. We have a board that meets the full group in nine days and will be asked, under oath if it comes to that, what we knew and when. And we have a press office fielding calls from two national outlets who've already decided this is a story about our AI chatbot, because we've spent eighteen months telling everyone how clever it is, and it's a much better headline than the truth, which is that a patient-matching routine nobody outside this building has ever heard of quietly stopped working." A flicker, there and gone. "I would like, at some point, for someone to be able to correct that story without it sounding like we're hiding behind the distinction."

"What do you want from me specifically."

"I want to know what happened. Genuinely. Not a version of what happened that photographs well." A small, deliberate pause — Maya filed it as the first honest beat in the sentence and the rest as scaffolding around it. "And I want it fast. The board will want a name it can put in a statement within the week. I'd like that name, if there has to be one, to actually be correct."

*If there has to be one.* Maya turned the phrase over once, quietly, the way she'd learned to turn over the first sentence any client said to her, because it usually told you more about what they were afraid of than anything that came after it.

"I don't work like that," Maya said. "I can tell you in a week what's true. I can't promise you it'll be a name."

Something moved behind Ashworth's composure — not much, a fraction of a degree, gone before Maya could be sure she'd seen it. "Understood. Bob will give you everything you need. Full access, full cooperation, nobody above you but me." She looked, for the first time, less like a CEO and more like a person doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy. "How long."

"Two weeks, if I'm not fighting anyone for access. Longer, if I am."

"You have until the twenty-third," Ashworth said, which Maya noted was eleven days, not fourteen, and did not comment on, because there would be time later to find out whose deadline that actually was — Ashworth's, or the board's, or somebody further up the chain who hadn't been in the room. "Robert will introduce you to Felix this afternoon. He runs the function this touched."

Kalu walked her out. In the corridor, away from the blinds and the six careful faces, he said, quieter than before, "For what it's worth — I don't think this is a person, Ms Reyes. I think it's bigger than that. I just don't think anyone in that room wants it to be, because a person is easier to fire than a system."

"That," Maya said, "is the most useful thing anyone's told me all morning."

She took the stairs down, all eleven floors, because she wanted the extra four minutes to decide, before she met Felix Adeyemi, whether she believed Kalu, or whether she'd simply liked being told what she already suspected — which was, she knew better than most people alive, a much easier thing to fall for than an outright lie.
