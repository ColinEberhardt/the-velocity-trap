# Chapter 9 — What He Trained For

*Draft — first pass.*

---

"Tell me the sentence again," Marcus said, the next morning, before either of them had finished their coffee. "The boy. Sam. You said he told you something and then you wrote it down word for word. I want the sentence, not your summary of it."

Maya didn't need to check her notebook. "*I know that it's wrong. I don't think I understand it well enough to know I should've been scared of it.*"

Marcus was quiet long enough that she started to wonder if he'd stopped listening, which she had learned by now meant he was listening harder than usual.

"That's not a sentence about a bug," he said finally. "That's a sentence about an entire career. I'd like to talk to him. Not about the code. About the sentence."

---

Sam looked marginally less like a man who hadn't slept, which Maya took as a good sign until she saw how carefully he was watching Marcus, the way you watch someone you've been told is important without yet knowing why.

"You're not in trouble," Marcus told him, before Sam had finished sitting down. "I want to be clear about that at the start, because I've noticed people don't hear anything useful I say afterward if I don't say it first." He didn't sit across from Sam so much as beside him, at an angle, which Maya recognised as deliberate — an interview conducted the way you'd conduct a conversation, not an interrogation. "Maya tells me you said something to her. That you understood the code was wrong, but not well enough to know you should have been scared of it. I'd like you to tell me what you meant."

Sam glanced at Maya, as though checking whether this was allowed, and she gave him nothing, because it wasn't her question to answer for him.

"I don't know how to explain it without sounding like I'm making excuses."

"Then sound like that. I'll decide separately whether it's an excuse."

Sam took a breath that seemed to go all the way down. "When I was at university, I thought I knew what I was training to become. A programmer. Like — a *real* one. The kind my lecturers were, some of them, the ones who'd tell you a story about a bug that took them three days to find fifteen years ago and you could tell it was the best three days of their week, even now, telling it. I wanted that. I taught myself to code when I was fourteen because I loved it, not because anyone made me — my parents didn't even really understand what I was doing, they just knew I was good at something and that felt like enough for all of us." He was talking faster now, the way people do once the actual thing finally starts coming out instead of the version they'd rehearsed. "And then I got here, and inside about three weeks I realised nobody was doing what I thought I'd trained to do. Nobody's really *writing* code, not the way I mean it. They're — steering it. Reviewing it. Prompting it and hoping. And I kept waiting to feel relieved, because everyone kept telling me this was more efficient, this was the future, and instead I just felt—" He stopped.

"Say the word," Marcus said, quietly. "Whatever it is. I promise you it's not a strange thing to feel."

"Sad," Sam said, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a weight he hadn't known he was allowed to put anywhere. "I felt sad. Like I'd trained for years for a job that stopped existing sometime while I wasn't looking, and I was too embarrassed to say that out loud to anyone, because it sounds like — it sounds like I'm grieving something everyone else has already moved past. My manager doesn't feel this. Josh doesn't feel this. He's just glad it's faster."

"How would you know that Josh doesn't feel it?"

Sam blinked. "He's never said anything."

"Neither had you, until about ninety seconds ago, and you'd apparently been carrying it since your first month here." Marcus let that sit. "I'm not telling you Josh feels the same thing. I'm telling you that you don't actually know he doesn't, and it might be worth noticing how confidently you'd assumed it."

Maya watched Sam turn that over, visibly, the way you turn over a stone you'd assumed was just a stone.

"Do you still code," Marcus asked. "For yourself. Not for work."

A pause, longer than the question should have needed, and something almost like embarrassment. "Most evenings, yeah. Stupid stuff. A tool that tracks my running times better than the app I paid for. A thing that reorganises my book notes. Nobody sees it. It's not — it's not important."

"It's the only part of your week where you're still the thing you trained to be," Marcus said, not unkindly, and not as a question. "That's not nothing. I'd hold onto that harder than you're currently holding onto it, if I were you."

Sam didn't have an answer for that, and didn't seem to need one.

"Last question," Marcus said, standing, done in the way he was always suddenly done. "When you shipped the code that broke, and it passed every test you ran — did any part of you feel, even for a second, like you didn't fully understand what you'd just approved?"

"Yes." No hesitation this time. "But everyone always feels that a bit. I thought that was just what the job felt like now. I thought that feeling was normal."

Marcus looked at Maya, briefly, and she understood the look perfectly, because she'd been having roughly the same thought for two days and hadn't yet found a clean way to say it.

*That's the actual finding,* the look said. *Not the PR number. That.*
