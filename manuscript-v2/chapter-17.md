# Chapter 17 — Reading Isn't Doing

The lantern over the door in the Clerkenwell mews was lit, and the cobbles were wet again. Inside it was as warm and small as before. The same eight stools stood at the same scrubbed counter. Aiko looked up from her board as they came in, and Yuki bowed and went back to whatever he was doing to a pan of rice.

"Twice in a week," Aiko said to Marcus. "It must be going badly."

"It's going quite well. That's the problem."

"Sit."

The first dish was three tiny curled squid, deep violet, on a smear of yellow mustard-miso.

"Hotaru-ika," Aiko said. "Firefly squid. From Toyama Bay. They come up from the deep water to spawn for about six weeks every spring, and they glow. The fishermen catch them at night." She set the plates down. "Next month, there won't be any."

Maya ate them. They tasted of the sea and of something sweet and dark. She found that for the first time in a week she was hungry.

For a while she let the meal happen, the way Marcus seemed to. She watched Aiko's hands and Yuki's hands, and the small exchanges between them that weren't quite words: a glance, a bowl set down, a cloth handed across before it was asked for. Eight diners again, tonight a group of four architects and two couples. Again the room had that quality of stillness that wasn't still at all.

She thought about the boardroom that morning. David on his screen and Ashworth's careful deferral. *Bring me something I can act on.*

"Can I ask you something?" Maya said, when Aiko came to their end of the counter to set down a bowl of clear soup with a single white flower of something floating in it.

"You can ask."

"When you closed the other restaurants. What did you actually change? Not the philosophy. The practice."

Aiko glanced at Marcus.

"She's learned from you," she said.

"She's learned from me," Marcus agreed. "You can't fob her off with the cucumber line. I've tried."

Aiko considered Maya for a moment, then wiped her hands and leaned on the counter.

---

"At Kaze," she said, "at the end, I had a hundred and sixty staff over four sites and a central kitchen. We were very successful. Good reviews, full every night. If you'd measured us the way my investors measured us, covers, revenue, speed of service, we were getting better every quarter." She paused. "And I didn't know what was happening on my own plates any more."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I couldn't taste them. There were too many. Each site had a head chef, each head chef had sous chefs, each sous chef had a brigade, and recipes came down from the central kitchen on cards. When something changed, a supplier or a recipe or a person, it went through five layers before it reached the plate. By the time it got there nobody could tell you why it had changed, or what else it had touched." She looked at Marcus. "The sesame was one change, in one recipe, in a building I didn't stand in. It took eleven days for anyone to find out what had happened, and that was with him asking."

"Twelve," Marcus said quietly.

"Twelve." Aiko didn't look away from Maya. "After that, I asked myself a question. How many people can I actually see? Really see. Not be told about, or have reports about, but see their hands while they work, and taste what they make before it goes out. And the honest answer was about two. Myself and one other." She gestured at Yuki, who didn't look up. "And how many diners can I actually see? See their faces when they eat, see if something isn't right? About eight. So that's what I built. Two cooks and eight seats. Every dish passes through my hands or past my eyes before it goes out. Every diner is close enough that I can see them. If something is wrong, I know within one course, not twelve days."

"Aiko," Marcus said, "that's a lovely story, and you've told it at three conferences. It's also impossible to check. *I couldn't taste the plates any more.* Every chef who's ever expanded says that. Give her something she can test."

"I was getting there," said Aiko, with a look of pure exasperation. "She asked for the practice, and you interrupted it." She turned back to Maya. "I counted things before and after. At Kaze, in the last year, we had a hundred and forty-one recorded food-safety near-misses across four sites. That's the ones that were written down. I suspect the real number was three times higher. Here, in twenty-eight years, I've had nine. I know every one of them by name and date. Complaints at Kaze averaged around thirty a month. Here, I can't tell you the number, because when something's wrong the diner tells me across the counter, and I fix it before the next course. It never becomes a complaint." She shrugged. "I also make much less money. I'm not pretending this is a business model for everybody. But you asked what I changed. I changed the distance. Between me and the hands, and between the hands and the plate, and between the plate and the person eating it." She paused. "And I stopped changing things all at once. At Kaze we changed everything every season, menus and suppliers and people, all in the same month, because the investors liked a relaunch. Here I change one thing at a time. One dish, or one supplier, or one habit. Never two in the same week. That way, when something goes wrong, I know which thing did it."

"The distance," Maya said.

"Everything that went wrong at Kaze happened in the distance." Aiko picked up her cloth again. "Something changed somewhere I couldn't see, and the person it hurt was somewhere else I couldn't see. Nobody in between was close enough to both."

---

Aiko went to plate the architects' next course. Maya sat very still.

She was thinking about Tariq's graph: the grey sprawl, the eleven orange nodes, Tariq dragging one of them and watching it spring back. She was thinking about what Marcus had said the week before, in front of Tariq's graph, which she'd nodded at and not quite heard. *You'd have to be close enough to notice, and nobody here is close enough to anything any more.*

She thought about Ruth at her desk under the window, who had been close enough to Escalation to find `normaliseRef` and write it in a box. Then they'd moved her to test tooling. She thought of Josh, with fourteen engineers and nine services, measured on a dashboard that showed the conveyor belt but not what was on it. Felix had two hundred services and a map of glowing dots. Ashworth had fourteen hospitals and a press office. And Mr Hale lay in a bed on Ward 4B at the far end of all of it, on the other side of a pre-op order set, a merged record, a config file, a Skill, a review, a register, a steering pack and a summariser. Nobody who could have seen the risk was anywhere near him, and nobody near him could see the risk. Helen Marsh had been eight seconds away and couldn't have known.

"It's not the code," Maya said slowly, mostly to herself. "It's not even the tools. It's that nobody's close enough to anything to notice when it drifts."

Marcus didn't say anything. He was watching her.

"Every safety mechanism at Meridian depended on someone being close," she said. "Close to the code, or the patient, or the decision. The phone call from the lab worked because a biochemist was close to the result and a nurse was close to the patient. Ruth's notebook worked because Ruth was close to the module. Even laziness worked, because it kept people close to the smallest possible piece of the system. Then everything that made Meridian faster moved people further away. The agents meant nobody had to be close to the code. The summariser meant nobody had to be close to the risk. The dashboards meant nobody had to be close to the work, only the numbers. And the team was fourteen people and nine services, so nobody was close to all of any of it." She took her glasses off. "You can't fix that with a better review process, or a better Skill, or a better summary. It's a distance problem. So you have to make the distance smaller."

"How?"

"Small teams." She said it before she'd fully thought it, then thought it and found it held. "Really small, three or four people. And they don't own a component, they own a whole thing, end to end, close enough that they can actually see it. One of them understands the risk and is allowed to say no. Someone is close to the person at the other end. In a hospital that's a clinician, someone like Helen Marsh, who knows what it's like when the pager doesn't go off. Two cooks and eight seats." She stopped. "Not for everything. But for the things where someone can die."

Marcus picked up his tea and drank, and put it down.

"Keep going," he said. "That's half of it."

---

Aiko came back with the next course: a single piece of fish on a dark plate, glistening, cut with a precision that was almost unsettling.

"Chu-toro," she said. "Medium fatty tuna."

Maya looked at it, and then down the counter at Yuki, who was wiping a board with his eyes lowered.

"Has he ever cut this?"

"No."

"Two years."

"Two years and three months." Aiko set the plate down. "He's watched me cut it perhaps two thousand times. He could describe every movement. If you asked him to write it down, he'd write it better than I could."

"Then why not let him?"

"Because watching isn't doing," Aiko said simply. "He knows what it looks like. He doesn't yet know what it feels like when it's going wrong. The knife tells you something just before the cut goes bad, a resistance, a change. You only learn to feel it by making a lot of bad cuts on things that don't matter. Cucumbers. Daikon. Cheap fish. Then one day, on the tuna, the knife tells you something and you stop, without knowing why. That's when you're ready. Not when you can describe it. When your hand stops on its own."

Maya stared at the fish.

She thought of Sam on the towpath, his hands out of his pockets. *I read it really carefully. And it didn't scare me. And it should have scared me.*

"That's Sam," she said.

Marcus turned his head.

"Sam read every line," Maya said. Her voice had changed, and she could hear it. "He told us. All afternoon, every line, more carefully than anyone else on that team. He's like Yuki. He's watched the master cut the fish two thousand times. He could describe it. He could probably write it down better than the agent." She put her hand flat on the counter. "But reading it isn't doing it. He never got to make the bad cuts. He never wrote Escalation himself, never hit the merged-record bug, never had to work out why at two in the morning. So when he saw `normaliseRef`, his hand didn't stop, because there was nothing in his hand to stop it. That's not because he's junior. Callum didn't stop either, and Callum's been there four years. It's because they've all been reading instead of doing for so long that nobody's hand stops any more." She looked at Marcus. "On the towpath I said reading carefully didn't save him, and I left it there, as if he'd just been unlucky. He wasn't unlucky. Reading carefully doesn't build the thing that would have saved him. Only doing builds it."

"And the tools?" Marcus said. "Are you going to tell Ashworth to take the agents away?"

"No." Maya shook her head. "That's the wrong lesson as well. Aiko doesn't refuse to use a sharp knife." She glanced at Aiko, who had stopped working and was listening. "The agents will get faster, and they should. Most of what they do is fine to do fast. But how fast you can produce something and how fast you build judgement aren't the same thing. They never will be. If you make one faster, you don't make the other one faster. You just make it easier to skip." She was thinking it through as she said it, and it held at each step. "So you have to protect the slow thing deliberately. Build it into how the team works, on purpose. Time for people to do the hard part themselves, badly, then less badly, on things that matter less. Maybe on the very thing they're going to be trusted with. Not because the tool can't do it. Because otherwise nobody ever learns what the tool's output should feel like when it's wrong."

The architects at the far end had gone quiet and were pretending not to listen.

Aiko looked at Maya for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the smallest movement.

"Yes," she said. "That's what I would say, if I were better with words."

She went back to her board. Before she did, she reached over and moved Yuki's cloth an inch to the left, without comment. Yuki adjusted it another half an inch and kept working.

---

Marcus didn't speak again until the end of the meal. When the last plate had gone and the tea had come, he took his notebook out of his canvas bag. He tore out a clean page and put it in front of Maya with a pen.

"Write that down," he said.

"Which bit?"

"All of it. The distance. Small teams that own the whole of something, with someone who can say no, and someone close to the patient. And the other thing, about reading and doing. Protecting the slow part deliberately." He pushed the pen towards her. "In your words, not mine. Not Aiko's. Write it the way you said it just now, before you start making it sound like a report."

"Marcus, I'll remember."

"You won't. Not the way you said it. You'll remember the conclusion and forget how you got there, and then on Thursday you'll stand up in front of David Strand with a conclusion and no road to it, and he'll knock it over." He tapped the paper. "You got there yourself. That's the only reason it's going to work. Write it down. You're going to need it on Thursday."

Maya picked up the pen.

At the far end of the counter, Aiko was cleaning her knife with a single slow stroke of the cloth. Yuki stood beside her, watching her hands.
