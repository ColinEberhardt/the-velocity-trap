# Characters

The core cast for the book — see `writing-plan.md` §4 for how this fits the wider process, and §3/§4a for the outline and teaching vehicle it needs to serve. Names are placeholders — easy to change, hard to leave un-decided. (The book's own title is settled: *The Velocity Trap*.) Each entry notes which [theme(s)](themes-to-dramatize.md) the character exists to carry, since in a fable every character is doing thematic work, not just plot work.

Maya, Marcus, Ruth, and Sam get a fuller backstory/voice pass below, since they carry most of the ~45,000-word page count (see the length decision in `writing-plan.md` §1) and need to stay consistent across ~20 chapters. Ashworth, Felix, Josh, and Tariq get lighter touch-ups — a couple of sharp, specific details each rather than full biographies, since they're deliberately leaner sketches appearing in a handful of scenes. None of this backstory needs to appear on the page directly; it exists so dialogue, reactions, and small details stay consistent with a real person underneath.

## Maya Reyes — the protagonist

Senior consultant at **Kestrel Advisory** (named in drafting, `../manuscript/chapter-02.md`), a mid-sized tech consultancy (a Scott-Logic-style firm), the "fixer" the board calls when something's gone wrong and needs to be understood fast. Very good at her job in a way that's become its own trap: her reputation was built on making problems disappear quietly and giving clients a clean, closeable answer — not on making them harder before they get easier. That's her flaw, and it's the engine of her arc: the story only works if she's tempted, credibly, to just find *a* cause, hand over a name, and go home, and has to actively fight that instinct as the investigation deepens. By Act 3 she risks her own contract and reputation to argue for the structural diagnosis over the easy scapegoat — the same choice the book is asking every character (and reader) to make.

**Backstory:** Trained originally in systems/mechanical engineering before moving into consulting — she thinks in systems and feedback loops by instinct, which is part of why she's good at this job even when it costs her. The formative event, never dramatized on the page but underlying everything she does: eight years ago, leading her first solo investigation (a manufacturing client, a serious near-miss), she found a plausible individual to blame, wrote a clean report, and it became the case study that made her career. Eighteen months later, the same uninvestigated systemic cause contributed to a fatality at a sister site. She has never told anyone this directly — not even Marcus, not yet — and it's the real source of her flaw: what looks like professional polish is partly suppressed guilt. This gives her arc a private stake that can surface once, briefly, late in the book (a confession scene, possibly to Marcus) without needing a flashback chapter.

**Voice and detail:** Precise, controlled, favours clinical language under stress; uses deflecting humour when uncomfortable rather than sincerity. Divorced (amicably; no children, by choice), close only to a sister she calls every Sunday — her one consistent tether outside work. Takes her glasses off when she's thinking hard, a small physical tic worth reusing as a beat.

## Marcus Kwan — the mentor

A semi-retired incident investigator with a public reputation for debunking AI hype — he's the person Maya's own consultancy calls in when a case is politically ugly enough that a fast, wrong answer would be actively dangerous. Teaches almost entirely through questions and through *demanding evidence*: his signature move is asking "how do you know that?" and making people go and check, rather than accepting a plausible-sounding story (this is where Colin's own newsletter voice — sceptical of unproven claims, quick to benchmark rather than believe — becomes a character trait rather than just an authorial stance; see the "note on voice, not plot" in `themes-to-dramatize.md`). Doesn't appear in Act 1; is pulled in once Maya realises the case is bigger than she can close alone.

**Backstory:** Decades ago, as a young investigator, he sat on a review board that signed off on a system as safe on the strength of impressive documentation — and it failed catastrophically, hurting people. That's the origin of "how do you know that?": he's spent the rest of his career refusing to trust a plausible-looking artefact again. His long friendship with Aiko Sato (see `writing-plan.md` §4a) comes from the same era: the incident he investigated at her old restaurant group was caused by exactly the same pattern — rapid, unplanned expansion outstripping the structure meant to hold it — and Aiko was the one person in that organisation who'd already been raising the alarm before it happened. He failed to protect her at the time, professionally and reputationally, and it's part of why he pushes Maya so hard, this time, to find and protect Ruth Okafor before it's too late. (This is a deliberate, quiet mirror — Marcus has already lived through a "Ruth" once and lost that fight; worth keeping as subtext rather than stating outright.)

**Voice and detail:** Dry, blunt, patient, but has zero tolerance for hand-waving; food and kitchen metaphors come naturally to him, seeded by decades of friendship with Aiko. Late sixties, plainly dressed despite his reputation, commands a room by speaking less rather than more.

## David — non-executive director (minor, recurring)

Named in drafting (`../manuscript/chapter-16.md`) — a board-level pressure source distinct from Ashworth, introduced when the full board (not just the executive team) gets involved in Act 3. Blunter and less sympathetic than Ashworth: pushes explicitly for naming Sam, then Josh, then threatens Maya's own engagement when she refuses both. Not fully resolved by the end of chapter 16 — "filing away a decision he intended to revisit regardless of what Friday brought" — a loose thread to either pay off (he tries again later) or let go in Act 4, worth a decision when drafting that far.

## Deborah Ashworth — Group CEO, the structural antagonist (part one)

Not a villain — she's under real pressure (regulator, press, shareholders) and wants the incident closed, explained, and *over* within days. Her demand for a fast, clean, individually-attributable cause is the book's clearest embodiment of the moral in a single character: she's not wrong that the organisation needs an answer quickly, she's wrong about what kind of answer actually prevents a repeat. Her arc is being talked down from a scapegoat to a structural fix without ever fully admitting she was going to do the wrong thing — she gets to claim the fix as her own idea by Act 4, which is realistic and fine.

**Voice and detail:** Rose through finance rather than a clinical or technical track; trained, disciplined crisis communicator — she talks in the register of a press statement even in private, which is its own tell. Took this role specifically to prove she could turn around a struggling organisation after a setback earlier in her career, which is why this incident threatens the exact narrative she's building for herself, not just the organisation's reputation.

## Felix Adeyemi — Chief Digital Officer, the structural antagonist (part two)

Owns a double mandate that turns out to be the book's clearest structural fault line: he's tasked with championing AI both in the *product* (patient- and clinician-facing chat interfaces, triage tools, the visible, board-fundable "innovation story") and in the *organisation* — how the engineering teams building all of it actually work (using AI coding tools as part of their day-to-day engineering practice). These are two different things, and Felix conflates them without realising it. He comes from product, not engineering, and is genuinely excellent at the first half of that mandate: he can tell a compelling story about what AI does for patients, he reads the market well, he's the reason Meridian Health Group is seen as ahead of its peers. (The incident that opens the book pointedly does *not* happen in the product he's proud of — that system is fine throughout. It happens in an unrelated, unglamorous internal system nobody outside engineering had ever thought about, which is why Felix has almost nothing to say about it when Maya asks — see the scope note in `writing-plan.md` §1.) He is fundamentally out of his depth on the second half of his mandate, and — crucially — doesn't know it. He has no felt sense of what it means to review code, what a codebase drifting apart actually looks like day to day, or why a skills library degrading silently is a real problem rather than an abstract one; when engineers try to explain it, it sounds to him like the kind of process complaint that slows things down, and he's spent his career learning to route around exactly that kind of complaint. So he manages the engineering side of the mandate the only way available to him: by proxy, through the metrics that are legible to him — velocity, output volume, how polished the documentation and planning artefacts look — which is precisely the surface where AI is best at looking good regardless of what's actually happening underneath (this is the book's most direct dramatization of theme 5, documentation's double edge, and the newsletter's "AI performance" idea: Felix isn't being lied to exactly, he's reading exactly the artefacts that were built to be read, and has no way to tell they're hollow). Carries theme 1 (he let usage fragment wildly and calls it empowerment — "let smart people find their own way" — because he has no framework for what *should* be managed here) and theme 2 (he signed off on the unaccounted spend because the output numbers looked good, and had no way to ask a sharper question than that). His arc: he doesn't get redeemed cleanly — he keeps his job, and never fully admits the depth of what he didn't understand, but by Act 4 he's visibly leaning on Maya and Ruth for the judgement he can't supply himself, which is as close to growth as a character like this gets.

**Voice and detail:** Talks in outcomes and stories rather than specifics — asked a precise technical question, he answers "what does this mean for patients," which is itself the tell of his blind spot. Came from a large consumer tech company before healthcare; genuinely believes in the mission, partly because of a bad healthcare experience in his own family, which is what makes him sympathetic rather than simply a bureaucratic obstacle.

## Josh Whitfield — engineering lead, the human face of the incentive problem

Runs the team whose code path is where the incident actually originated. Not incompetent or malicious — he did what the organisation was implicitly rewarding: maximum visible output, minimum friction. Carries theme 1's volume-chasing extreme and theme 2 (cost pressure pushed his team toward the cheapest, fastest path). His discomfort when this gets named directly to his face — "I was just doing what got rewarded" — is one of the book's clearest dramatizations of the moral (organisational incentives, not individual failure).

**Voice and detail:** Talks constantly in shipping/velocity metrics; visibly anxious discussing anything that isn't a number he can point to. Under real personal financial pressure (mortgage, young kids), which is part of why he chased visible output as hard as he did — survival, not ambition.

## Ruth Okafor — senior engineer, the vindicated whistleblower

Leads the (fictional, not interview-sourced — see `themes-to-dramatize.md` theme 1) faction who refused to let AI near patient-facing work on principle, and had specifically warned about the failure mode that caused the incident, in writing, months earlier. Sidelined and quietly resented before the incident as "the person holding the programme back." Carries theme 1's principled-refusal thread and a hard edge of theme 9 (a career cost paid for being right too early). Her vindication and folding-in as the safety backbone of the new team structure in Act 4 is the clearest emotionally satisfying payoff in the book — worth protecting in the outline.

**Backstory:** Spent her first fifteen years in medical device firmware (pacemakers, infusion pumps) — a discipline with genuinely rigorous, regulator-driven engineering practice (design controls, verification and validation) — before moving into healthcare software, where she was startled to find "software" treated far more casually than "device," and then watched AI adoption erode what rigor remained. Her caution isn't reflexive technophobia; it's transplanted professional rigor from a stricter discipline, which is exactly why it reads as credible rather than ideological once anyone actually listens to her. She's also a parent — a teenage daughter — and part of her caution is explicitly parental: she thinks about consequences the way a parent does, "what if this were my own child's care."

**Voice and detail:** Measured, precise, technical/regulatory register; doesn't raise her voice even when frustrated, which is part of why colleagues misread calm precision as stubbornness. Keeps meticulous written records as a matter of professional habit — which is exactly how her ignored warning survives to be found later.

## Sam Ilyas — junior engineer, ground-zero of the incident

Recent computer science graduate, embedded in Josh's team, technically the person who prompted and shipped the specific vulnerable pattern — without fully understanding it, because nothing in his onboarding ever forced him to. His backstory is where theme 9 stops being an abstract industry thesis and becomes personal: he went into a CS degree expecting to become what he thought a "real programmer" was — the craft his older colleagues and his own lecturers had described, line-by-line mastery, the satisfaction of building something yourself — and graduated to find the ground had moved while he wasn't looking. His first weeks on the job are the moment he realises nobody around him is really *writing* code in the sense he trained for; they're prompting, reviewing, steering, and the actual authorship — the part he'd spent three years learning to do and looked forward to doing — has quietly become someone else's job, if it's anyone's job at all. He finds this unsettling and genuinely sad, not just professionally inconvenient — a grief he doesn't have the vocabulary for yet and nobody around him seems to feel as sharply (echoing the newsletter's "craft/identity grief" material — see theme 9 — but arriving *before* he's built anything to grieve, which is a harder, lonelier version of the same loss than a mid-career engineer mourning a craft they at least got to practice). This is what makes him vulnerable to shipping code he doesn't understand: he's not lazy or careless, he's a highly capable junior operating in a role that no longer resembles the one he trained and hoped for, with no one senior enough noticing he'd never actually hit the wall that used to force real understanding. His arc across the investigation is the emotional low point of the book (he initially believes the incident is entirely his fault, which briefly confirms his fear that he was never a "real" engineer to begin with) and his re-framing — this was a systemic failure that used him as the point of failure, not a personal one, and his grief about the craft is legitimate rather than something to just get over — is one of Maya's clearest wins.

**Backstory:** First in his family to go to university; his parents are proud he's "gone into computers." He chose CS because he genuinely loved the puzzle-solving craft of programming from his early teens, taught himself to code recreationally before ever studying it formally — which is what makes the loss sharper: this wasn't just a chosen career path, it was a love, and he's arrived to find the thing he loved doing for fun has quietly stopped being a viable professional practice. He still codes for himself at home in the evenings, a private refuge nobody at work knows about — a small, unstated echo of Tariq Farouk's "software for one" thread (theme 11), worth keeping as a quiet parallel rather than spelling out.

**Voice and detail:** Eager to please, over-explains and over-apologises, hedges everything ("I think," "maybe," "sorry if this is wrong") — visible imposter syndrome in his speech patterns, which is itself a clue nobody around him has been reading correctly.

## Tariq Farouk — platform engineer, the texture/counterweight character

A quieter supporting role, there to carry the two newsletter-only themes that don't fit naturally into the main investigation: he maintains a small open-source library that's tangled up in the incident's blast radius (theme 10 — open source strain, copyright/maintenance questions surface briefly here), and, entirely separately, has spent evenings building a small "software for one" tool for his own use that has nothing to do with any of this (theme 11 — the book's deliberate tonal counterweight). His closing-chapter cameo, still quietly building small useful things for their own sake, is the moment the book allows itself to be a little hopeful without undercutting the seriousness of everything else.

**Voice and detail:** Quiet, understated, far more comfortable talking about his code and tools than about himself. His open-source library started years ago as a personal hobby project and unexpectedly became infrastructure other people now depend on without knowing his name — a small private irony he carries, and a direct, human echo of themes 10 and 11 sitting inside one character rather than needing two.

## Yuki — Aiko's apprentice at Shun (minor, background)

Named in drafting (`../manuscript/chapter-08.md`) — the second half of Shun's two-person kitchen. Two years in, not yet trusted with the tuna. Purely texture: a living example of the shokunin apprenticeship principle (§4a) on the page, not a character with lines or an arc of his own.

## Robert Kalu — Meridian's general counsel (minor, recurring)

Not part of the core roster, but Maya's main point of contact and the one person at Meridian who says the honest thing out loud in chapter 2 ("I don't think this is a person... a person is easier to fire than a system"). Functions as an ally-of-convenience for Maya throughout the investigation — someone who wants the true answer for legal-exposure reasons that happen to align with her own for better ones. Referred to as "Kalu" generally and "Bob" once, by Ashworth, establishing the nickname without needing to explain it.

## Priya — junior colleague at Kestrel Advisory (minor, recurring)

Not part of the core roster — a brief presence bookending the book rather than a character with an arc. Appears in chapter 2 (three years into the firm, present when Maya first gets the call) and again in chapter 24 (now about to lead her own first solo investigation), where Maya writes her the report distilling the book's closing framework rather than just talking her through it. The gap between those two appearances is the point: Priya is where the lesson goes next.

## A note on cast size

This is 7 named characters plus the protagonist and mentor (9 total), plus Aiko Sato and Priya as minor recurring presences outside the core roster — appropriate for the book's tighter, faster-paced investigation frame rather than a wider ensemble (a "three pods" structure with a bigger cast was considered and set aside — see `alternatives-considered.md`). With the length target set at ~45,000 words (see `writing-plan.md` §1), that cast size stays as-is but the *weight* given to each character doesn't split evenly: Maya, Marcus, Ruth, and Sam carry most of the page count; Josh and Tariq are deliberately leaner sketches, present to do their thematic job in a scene or two rather than to carry a full arc. If the "three pods" ensemble idea (`alternatives-considered.md`) ever gets folded in, Josh's team could be split into two contrasting pods without much rework, but that would push against the novella length and isn't needed now.
