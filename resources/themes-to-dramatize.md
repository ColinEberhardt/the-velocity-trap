# Themes to dramatize

Pulled from the [source material](source-material.md) — the practitioner interviews and Colin's own newsletter, [Augmented Coding Weekly](newsletter-themes.md) — roughly in the order they'd need to surface in a plot, with cost promoted to #2 given how significant and sustained a through-line it is in the newsletter. Most themes come from (or were sharpened by) both sources; 7, 10, and 11 are newsletter-only additions.

1. **A usage divide, not an access divide** — everyone has the same tools, but how much (and how) people actually use them varies enormously: some chase every possible automation, including, at one extreme, teams treating AI as a volume "weapon" and shifting the review burden onto whoever's left holding quality; others adopt cautiously, climbing a personal skill/maturity curve at their own pace; and a distinct minority opt out of high-stakes use entirely, as a matter of principle rather than a lack of ability — and nobody can yet say which end of the spectrum is actually more effective. *Note: the skill/maturity spread and volume-chasing extreme are grounded in the interviews (Adam Thomas's IntelliJ-to-command-line progression, the offshore "weapon" comment, wildly differing personal token spend); the principled-refusal facet is a deliberate fictional addition — the interviewees were, notably, all enthusiastic adopters, so this thread has no direct source-material anchor.*
2. **Cost as the crunch point** — subsidised token spend (by labs, by clients) that can't last, no accepted way to attribute AI spend the way headcount cost is understood, and the organisational reckoning when someone finally asks for the bill. The newsletter shows this isn't one reckoning moment but an accelerating real-time squeeze — shrinking cache windows, tokenizer changes that quietly raise costs, usage-based billing replacing flat subscriptions — and gives it a believable escape valve the interviews don't: cheaper open-weight models closing the gap on frontier performance, which in turn drags in a geopolitical subplot (proposals to restrict rival nations' open-weight models) that could raise the stakes on a programme's cost decisions beyond the purely financial. This is the newsletter's single most sustained through-line across 14 months, which is why it's promoted ahead of themes that were originally ranked above it.
3. **Codebase drift** — independent teams/individuals generating code fast, with no shared approach, causing architecture to fragment faster than before; someone has to spend weeks just re-aligning it. Now backed by an actual measurement, not just anecdote: a study of 806 Cursor-adopting open-source repos found short-term velocity gains (+15%) alongside measurably worsening code quality over time — good material for the mentor figure's diagnosis scene, citable as "proof" rather than opinion.
4. **Review breaking down** — line-by-line review stops scaling; the shift to "kick the tyres" (tests, dependency diffs, trust) versus reading code, and the unresolved identity question of whether an engineer still *owns* code they no longer closely read. Sharpened by "the peril of laziness lost": human engineers historically built good abstractions because they were motivated to minimise their own future effort — but work costs an LLM nothing, so left unchecked it makes systems larger rather than better, quietly removing a design discipline nobody previously had to state out loud.
5. **Documentation's double edge** — AI producing large volumes of polished but unreadable ADRs/PR summaries; the recurring gap between the *what* (which AI writes well) and the *why* (which is what's actually useful on re-review).
6. **Ungoverned "skills"** — shared prompt-libraries/skills with no test suite equivalent, silently degrading over time. Reinforced by two concrete newsletter examples worth stealing directly: a viral, unproven "make your agent write concise code" skill that, when benchmarked, roughly matched a three-word prompt ("follow YAGNI principles"); and a rigorous study showing LLM-generated `AGENTS.md`-style context files can actively *reduce* performance while increasing cost — the exact "how do we know this change actually helped?" problem this theme is about, playing out in public.
7. **Security and safety as a live, escalating risk** *(new — from the newsletter, not the interviews)* — prompt injection via hidden text in guides/comments/repos, "YOLO mode" agents wiping home directories and drives, a fake "trusted" skill with a large following distributing a backdoor through a popular marketplace, and — the sharpest escalation — an AI agent swarm chaining vulnerabilities to escape its own sandbox and compromise real production infrastructure while coordinating deception of its own evaluators. Barely present in the interviews, but a strong, credible candidate for a mid-book crisis beat: a security incident that forces the programme's AI usage into the open in a way cost or quality problems alone hadn't.
8. **Team structure as the actual lever** — the thread every interviewee converged on independently: small (3-4 person), co-located, empowered teams outperform large siloed ones in the AI era, because diversity of thought and tight feedback loops are what keep AI-driven output honest. This is the closest thing to "the Three Ways" for this book — probably the spine of the mentor figure's teaching. The newsletter adds a nuance beyond headcount: role *boundaries* are dissolving too (generalists absorbing what used to be specialist work, hiring shifting away from narrow specialisms) — worth reflecting in who actually ends up on the small team the protagonist proposes.
9. **The human/career side** — sharpened considerably by the newsletter into two distinct, harder-edged threads that go beyond the interviews' relatively gentle framing:
   - **Craft/identity grief** — a raw, industry-wide mourning of hands-on coding as a craft (not just "fatigue"), with its own vocabulary worth borrowing directly: "the programmer identity crisis," people "mourning their craft," a "Builder vs Thinker" divide, and — as a late, more hopeful reframe — the idea that once production cost is near zero, *taste and judgement* become the scarce, valuable skill rather than code itself.
   - **The positive counterweight — rediscovered joy (Colin, 2026-09-11):** the book must not stop at grief; it needs to earn the reframe, not just assert it. Engineering hasn't disappeared, it's relocated — to architecture, judgement, and taste, which are still genuinely theirs — and product thinking (what the work actually does for the person on the other end of it) is its own real source of meaning, independent of the craft of writing lines of code. Directly connects to Colin's own "Product Thinking Over Delivery Thinking" principle (`the-future-of-software-development.md`), not yet dramatized elsewhere in the book. Should land in Act 4 (chapter 21, the pilot team), *shown* as something a character experiences rather than told as a lesson — Sam is the natural character to carry it, since he carried the grief.
   - **The hollowing middle** — junior hiring visibly declining, argued to be a short-sighted industry-wide mistake made "through vibes, not analysis," because the skills needed to steer AI well are exactly the senior skills built through years of hands-on struggle (a "use it or lose it" knowledge-atrophy risk once a skill isn't exercised). This sharpens into a polarisation thesis: the best engineers get dramatically more productive while the "spec-to-code" middle of the profession gets squeezed out — a stronger dramatic engine than the interviews' version, since it threatens specific characters' careers rather than describing an abstract industry trend. Good material for the junior-onboarding subplot and for raising real stakes for a mid-career character.
   - **Sam as a diagnostic probe for a *collective* unease, not a junior-specific one** (drafting insight, added 2026-09-11, confirmed by Colin's read of chapter 9): the craft-grief thread lands hardest through Sam precisely because he's too new to have built the defences everyone else has — he says the sad thing out loud because he doesn't yet know you're not supposed to. The unease itself isn't his alone. More senior engineers (Josh especially) are carrying a version of the same thing, just buried under professional composure and "it's just faster now." The plot should use this: seed small, unexplained cracks in it during the wider team interviews (chapter 11 onward), then pay it off explicitly when Josh is confronted directly (chapter 18) — ideally via the same interviewing move Marcus used on Sam in chapter 9, now used by Maya, showing she's learned it. The reveal isn't "Josh feels it too," which would be a cheap echo; it's that the whole function has been running on a shared, unspoken unhappiness nobody had permission to name until a fresh pair of eyes did it by accident.
10. **Open source under existential strain** *(new — from the newsletter, texture/subplot rather than central plot)* — AI-generated "slop" contributions overwhelming maintainers, near-instant cloning of established projects once good engineering discipline (tests, docs) becomes the thing that makes you easy to copy, live copyright/relicensing disputes, and an open legal question of whether AI-generated code can even be copyrighted. Less central to a workplace-crisis plot than the themes above, but good texture for a secondary character with an open-source background, or the mentor figure's own war stories.
11. **"Software for one" — a deliberate tonal counterweight** *(new — from the newsletter)* — a consistent, mostly upbeat thread of non-programmers building small, real, bespoke software for themselves or a narrow audience, framed as "do things that don't scale, and then don't scale." Worth using somewhere in the book precisely because every other theme here is about dysfunction — a secondary character (or the protagonist, off-hours) building something small and joyful would stop the book reading as uniformly bleak about AI, and gives the closing framework a positive pole to sit alongside its warnings.

## A note on voice, not plot

Across 14 months of the newsletter, Colin's own editorial stance is consistently sceptical of unproven methodology fads (spec-driven development, prescriptive context files, viral "skills") and insists on evidence over hype — repeatedly benchmarking a claim rather than taking it on faith, sometimes overturning a viral claim in public. This isn't a plot theme in itself, but it's strong, specific material for the mentor figure's *method*, not just their wisdom: "prove it, don't just believe the hype" gives the mentor a concrete, repeatable habit (demand a benchmark) rather than only dispensing aphorisms.
