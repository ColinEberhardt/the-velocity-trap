# A typical novel-writing process

A general reference for how authors commonly move from idea to finished book. Not a fixed recipe — most writers loop back through these stages rather than moving through them once — but useful as a checklist for where this project currently sits and what's still ahead.

## 1. Premise and concept

Boil the book down to one or two sentences: who it's about, what world/situation they're in, and what's at stake. For a business fable specifically, this usually also means naming the real-world problem the story exists to explain — the "moral" has to be decided before the plot, because the plot is built to prove it.

## 2. Research

Gather the raw material the story will draw on: real events, interviews, data, personal experience. For business fables in particular, credibility depends on the tensions in the story being recognisable to readers who live the real version — invented conflict reads as invented.

## 3. Outline / structure

Most novel-length work benefits from some structural skeleton before drafting, even a loose one. Common approaches:

- **Three-act structure** — setup, confrontation, resolution.
- **Beat sheet** — a chapter-by-chapter (or scene-by-scene) list of what happens and why, often mapped against a known structural model (e.g. Save the Cat, the Hero's Journey, or — for business fables — the "crisis → mentor → transformation → distilled lesson" shape).
- **Reverse-outlining from the ending** — deciding the climax and the takeaway first, then working backward to figure out what has to happen earlier to earn it. Business fables often work this way, since the "lesson" is the actual deliverable and the plot exists to dramatize it.

Key decisions usually made at this stage: point of view, tense, timeline, and the core cast (protagonist, antagonist or antagonistic force, mentor/foil figures).

## 4. Character development

Beyond the outline, most authors develop characters in more depth than what appears on the page — backstory, motivation, voice, flaws — so that dialogue and decisions stay consistent across hundreds of pages. For a fable, characters often double as stand-ins for a real-world role or archetype (the overwhelmed manager, the political operator, the wise outsider), so it helps to be explicit about which real tension each character exists to carry.

## 5. Drafting

Writing the manuscript start to finish (or scene by scene, not necessarily in order). The dominant advice across working authors is to keep the first draft moving rather than editing as you go — get the whole shape down, then fix it — since early over-editing is a common way novels stall permanently. A typical first draft is treated as "clay, not sculpture."

## 6. Revision (structural/developmental edit)

Once a full draft exists, step back and assess the big things: does the plot hold together, does the pacing work, do the characters change convincingly, does the ending actually deliver on the premise. This pass often involves cutting or reordering whole scenes/chapters, not line editing. Many authors set the draft aside for a period first, to get enough distance to see it freshly.

## 7. Line editing and copyediting

Sentence-level work: clarity, voice, consistency, rhythm, dialogue tags, repeated words/tics. Copyediting adds grammar, continuity checks (does a character's eye colour change halfway through), and consistency of names/timelines/facts.

## 8. Feedback — beta readers / critique

Getting the manuscript in front of readers who represent the intended audience, before it's "finished," to catch what the author can no longer see: confusing sections, dead spots, characters who don't land, whether the ending actually pays off. For a business fable this is especially valuable with readers from the target professional audience — do the dramatized tensions actually ring true to someone living the real version.

## 9. Final proofread

A last pass focused purely on catching remaining typos, formatting errors, and small inconsistencies — done after content changes have stopped, ideally by someone (or some process) other than whoever did the earlier edits.

## 10. Publishing / release

Decisions about format and route to readers: traditional publishing (agent → publisher), self-publishing, or in this case likely a standalone digital release or booklet given the external-thought-leadership goal. This stage also covers framing — title, cover, blurb, and where/how it's shared — which for a piece meant to land with an industry audience matters as much as the writing itself.
