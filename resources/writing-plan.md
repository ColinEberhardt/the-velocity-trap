# Writing plan

The working document for this project, structured against the stages in [`novel-writing-process.md`](novel-writing-process.md). Each section below is the deliverable for that stage. Filled in up to and including outline/structure; later stages are placeholders to fill in as the project progresses.

## 1. Premise and concept

**Title (confirmed 2026-09-14): The Velocity Trap.**

**Premise:** A senior investigator/fixer at a mid-sized tech consultancy is called in by the board of **Meridian Health Group**, a large private healthcare group, within days of a serious clinical safety incident traced to a failure in an unglamorous, unremarkable piece of internal software. The brief is blunt: find out how it happened, and whether it can happen again, before regulators or the press get there first. What looks at first like one engineer's bad decision unravels into something structural: wildly uneven personal AI usage across engineering teams — some burning thousands of pounds a month chasing every possible automation, others barely spending pennies, and a faction who'd refused to let AI near anything patient-facing on principle and had warned about exactly this; AI spend that had quietly pushed teams toward the cheapest, fastest path; codebases that had drifted apart faster than anyone could reconcile; and a mountain of polished-but-unreadable AI-written documentation that, it turns out, actually contained the warning nobody had trusted enough to read. Guided by a mentor figure who has investigated this exact failure mode before, elsewhere, the protagonist has to fight the board's appetite for a scapegoat and make the case for a structural diagnosis instead — one that could prevent the next incident rather than just answer for this one.

**A note on scope, corrected 2026-09-11 — AI-as-product vs. AI-in-the-SDLC:** these are two different things in this book, and the plot must keep them separate or it muddles the actual thesis. Meridian's AI *product* — the patient- and clinician-facing chat/triage interface Felix Adeyemi champions (see `characters.md`) — is background/character texture: it establishes his pride, his blind spot, and Meridian's self-image as an AI-forward organisation. **It is not what fails, and it is not the subject of the investigation.** The incident, and everything Maya and Marcus investigate across all eleven [themes](themes-to-dramatize.md), concerns AI used as an *engineering tool* — coding agents writing, reviewing, and documenting software — inside an ordinary, unremarkable internal system nobody outside engineering has ever thought about. That contrast is deliberate and should sharpen, not soften, Felix's blind spot: he is fluent and proud talking about the flashy AI product that is *fine*, and has almost nothing to say about the boring system that actually broke — because "AI in engineering" was never something his mandate meant to him. See `characters.md`'s Felix entry and the chapter-level beat sheet below (chapters 1 and 3 especially) for how this plays out on the page.

**The moral (what the book exists to prove):** AI doesn't fail these organisations — it succeeds too well at the wrong thing (raw volume, plausible-looking output, individual speed) while the surrounding structure, process, and team design stay unchanged, so the result is acceleration of existing dysfunction rather than a fix for it. This is especially true of very large organisations, where you cannot even see both ends of the loop — engineer, product owner, end user — let alone join them up. The way out isn't more/better AI tooling; it's redesigning how small, empowered, trusted humans and AI actually work together — landing on a small, memorable set of principles by the end, the way *The Phoenix Project* lands on the Three Ways. The investigation framing sharpens this into a specifically preventive lesson: not just "AI needs restructuring to help," but "this is how you find out *before* the incident, not after."

**Length target: a novella, roughly 45,000 words (~150–180 pages), not a full-length novel.** *The Phoenix Project* runs to ~110,000 words, but that reflects a slow-burn ensemble turnaround; this book's investigation pacing is better served by momentum than bulk, and a shorter book is more likely to actually get read start-to-finish by a busy engineering-leader audience — which matters more here than hitting a traditional novel-length shelf convention. The practical consequence for the cast (`characters.md`): not every character gets a full arc at this length. Maya, Marcus, Ruth, and Sam carry most of the weight; Josh and Tariq stay leaner supporting sketches rather than fully developed arcs, and themes 5, 6, 10, and 11 (texture/grace-notes) land in a scene or two of dialogue each rather than dedicated subplots.

## 3. Outline / structure

The four-act shape: **crisis → diagnosis → transformation → distilled-lesson**, mirroring *The Phoenix Project*. The story is investigation-led and fast-paced — it opens cold on a security incident (theme 7) rather than holding it in reserve, and the protagonist is brought in specifically because of the incident, not as a delivery lead sent to rescue a struggling programme; the "AI success story masking dysfunction" framing lives inside what the investigation uncovers, not the opening pitch. Theme numbers refer to `themes-to-dramatize.md` (cost is theme 2). Two other structures were explored before settling on this one — see [`alternatives-considered.md`](alternatives-considered.md), kept as a reference bank (in particular, its "three pods" ensemble device is still a good source of secondary-character/subplot material for later).

- **Act 1 — The crisis (the incident):** Cold open on the security incident itself (or its immediate aftermath) — patient-facing systems affected, the board demanding answers within days, not months. The protagonist is brought in as an outside investigator. Early interviews reveal the uneven AI usage divide (*theme 1*) and the fact that some engineers had been raising exactly this risk and were ignored (*theme 1*'s principled-refusal strand, now with real stakes attached from page one).
- **Act 2 — The diagnosis (the investigation):** Peeling back the incident reveals it wasn't one mistake but a stack of them: unaccounted AI cost pressure had pushed teams toward the cheapest, fastest path (*theme 2*); codebase drift meant nobody had a full picture of the system that failed (*theme 3*); review had stopped catching this class of problem (*theme 4*); the documentation that should have flagged the risk existed but was AI-written noise nobody trusted enough to act on (*theme 5*); the skill/prompt-library that generated the vulnerable pattern had degraded silently over months (*theme 6*). The mentor figure is introduced as someone who's investigated this exact failure mode before, elsewhere.
- **Act 3 — The reckoning:** The board wants a scapegoat; the protagonist has to fight for a structural diagnosis instead of a punitive one, risking their own credibility to do it. The fix — small, empowered, mixed teams with real ownership (*theme 8*) — is proposed not as an improvement but as the only way to make this kind of incident visible *before* it happens next time. The engineers who'd raised the alarm are vindicated and folded into the new structure. A human/career cost of the incident itself (someone's career or confidence genuinely damaged, fairly or not) sharpens theme 9.
- **Act 4 — The turn:** The new structure is implemented under real regulatory/board scrutiny, not just internal ambition — raising the stakes on whether it actually works. It does, visibly, in time to matter. Closing chapter: the distilled framework, framed explicitly around prevention and evidence over hype (ties directly to the mentor-voice note on scepticism of unproven fads in `themes-to-dramatize.md`) rather than pure productivity.

## 4a. The teaching vehicle — Marcus's "Parts Unlimited"

*The Phoenix Project* has a specific, memorable device: Erik doesn't lecture Bill about Theory of Constraints, he takes him to walk the floor of a manufacturing plant and makes him *see* WIP piling up at a bottleneck station. The lesson lands because it's concrete, physical, and from completely outside Bill's own domain, so he can't argue with his own expertise. This book's equivalent is a small restaurant kitchen, brought to life through East Asian culinary philosophy — not as set dressing, but as the actual content of the lesson. Four other candidate vehicles were explored before landing here — see [`alternatives-considered.md`](alternatives-considered.md), kept as a reference bank (the M&M-conference idea in particular remains a good candidate for a single light touchpoint elsewhere in the book, if a second, darker-toned counterpoint to the kitchen is wanted later).

**The restaurant:** a small, exceptional restaurant called **Shun** (旬 — the Japanese word for a food's fleeting moment of peak season; "eating with the seasons"), run by chef-owner **Aiko Sato**. Aiko spent years running a large, successful, chaotic restaurant group — multiple sites, constant expansion, a brigade so big she'd stopped knowing everyone's name — before deliberately closing all but one site and shrinking to a single eight-seat counter. That backstory does real work: she's not a mystic who's never known scale, she's someone who tried scale, watched it hollow out the thing she cared about, and made a considered choice to go small on purpose. Marcus has known her for years — he investigated a serious kitchen incident at her old restaurant group early in his career, and it's part of why she made the change; that shared history is what earns him a standing table.

**The philosophy, made concrete rather than decorative:**
- **Shokunin (craftsman spirit)** — total, unglamorous devotion to a narrow craft, built through years of repetition. Aiko's apprentices aren't allowed to touch the star ingredient for their first year; they do prep, they watch, they fail at small things safely. This is the vehicle's most direct thematic payload: it's a concrete, dramatized version of exactly what Sam Ilyas never got to have (see §4) — the years of "boring" repetition that used to be how understanding got built, which AI has quietly made it possible to skip.
  **Sharper version (Colin, 2026-09-11 — must land explicitly by the time the restaurant/incident parallel solidifies, i.e. chapter 17, not before):** the point isn't only that AI makes it *possible* to skip the repetition — it's that AI's instant output actively discourages the slow practice judgement is built from, even for someone doing everything "right." Watching or carefully reviewing a result you didn't struggle to produce doesn't teach you what struggling to produce it would have — this is already dramatized empirically in chapter 9 (Sam *did* read the code carefully; it still didn't give him the judgement to be scared of it). Aiko's apprentices could watch a master plate a dish perfectly in seconds and learn almost nothing from it; they only learn from the years of doing it themselves, slowly, badly, then less badly. The prescription that falls out of this isn't "slow AI down" — it's that teams must *intentionally* protect slow, effortful practice as a discipline, on purpose, regardless of how fast the tools get, because speed of output and speed of judgement-building are not the same axis and never will be. This needs to be the thing Maya actually articulates when the parallel lands in chapter 17 (see the beat sheet), not just a mood the chapter gestures at.
- **Kaizen (continuous small improvement)** — Aiko's kitchen changes constantly in tiny ways and almost never in large ones; she's suspicious of any "big fix" proposed all at once. A direct, usable line for Marcus to hand Maya when the board wants a single sweeping restructure.
- **Ichigo ichie (一期一会 — "one time, one meeting")** — the idea that each service, each dish, each encounter is unrepeatable and deserves full attention. Marcus uses this against the culture of skimming AI-generated summaries and "TL;DR" documentation (theme 5) — Aiko's kitchen has no shortcuts for *attention itself*, no matter how efficient the prep gets.
- **Effortless mastery that isn't effortless** — from the pass, service looks calm, almost slow; up close, it's the product of extreme rehearsal. A clean counter to Josh Whitfield's team, where things *look* fast (output, velocity) but are actually out of control underneath — the inverse relationship between how something looks and what's actually happening is the whole point of the investigation plot.

**Guardrail — avoid it reading as exoticised mysticism:** the risk with this material is real: an outsider marvelling at "wisdom of the East" can read as shallow or othering if it's not grounded in specific, concrete craft. The fix is Marcus's own established character trait (see §4, and the "note on voice, not plot" in `themes-to-dramatize.md`): he's the evidence-over-hype sceptic, and that scepticism should fire *in-scene* — he's visibly impatient the first time Aiko says something that sounds like a fortune cookie, and it's only when she reframes it in concrete operational terms (what she actually does differently, what she measured when she downsized, what specifically broke at scale) that he — and Maya, and the reader — take it seriously. That turns a potential weakness into a character beat that's consistent with who Marcus already is.

**Staging, across the book:** a first visit early (Act 1 or the Act 1/2 turn) establishes Shun and Aiko as a place Marcus goes to think, without yet drawing the parallel explicitly — Maya is mostly baffled why they're there. A second visit in Act 3, once Maya has enough of the investigation's shape to see it herself, is where the parallel lands hard — ideally Maya makes the connection before Marcus states it, which is a stronger beat than him lecturing her. A short callback in Act 4 (a final meal, a single line from Aiko) closes it out, mirroring how Parts Unlimited recurs across *The Phoenix Project* rather than appearing once.

**Cast note:** Aiko Sato is a minor, few-scene character — not part of the core 9-person roster in §4, in keeping with the novella length budget (see the "note on cast size" above).

## 4b. Chapter-level beat sheet

A first-pass chapter list for the ~45,000-word novella (see §1), turning §3's four acts into scenes. Targets ~24 short chapters averaging ~1,800–2,000 words — a deliberately brisk, thriller-compatible chapter length, not the longer chapters typical of a full novel. **POV decision (suggested): close third-person on Maya throughout, single-POV**, like Bill Palmer in *The Phoenix Project* — keeps the novella tight and means every scene has to happen in front of her, which is good discipline for an investigation plot. Open to revisiting if a chapter (e.g. Sam's or Ruth's) badly wants its own POV.

### Act 1 — The crisis (chapters 1–6, ~11,000 words)
1. Cold open on the incident itself, or its immediate chaos — a failure in a mundane, unglamorous internal system (not the AI product — see the scope note in §1), affecting real patient safety. Establishes stakes viscerally before Maya even appears.
2. Maya gets the call; boardroom scene with Deborah Ashworth — blunt, days not months, an implicit demand for a name.
3. Maya arrives on site; Felix Adeyemi proudly walks her through the AI *product* success story — then goes notably vague and dismissive the moment she asks about the actual system that failed ("that's plumbing, that's Josh's world"), sharpening his blind spot rather than implicating the product he's proud of. First glimpse of the usage divide (theme 1).
4. Maya meets Josh Whitfield's team, including Sam Ilyas; first look at the code path under suspicion.
5. Maya finds Ruth Okafor's written warning from months earlier, buried and ignored — the principled-refusal thread (theme 1) gets real stakes.
6. Act break: Maya realises this is bigger than one bad decision and calls in Marcus Kwan.

### Act 2 — The diagnosis (chapters 7–15, ~16,000 words)
7. Marcus arrives; his method established — "how do you know that?" — and his and Maya's dynamic set up.
8. First visit to Shun: Marcus takes Maya there to think, unexplained; she's baffled why they're eating dinner instead of working the case.
9. Interview with Sam: his backstory surfaces — the CS grad who expected to become a "real programmer" and found everyone just prompting instead.
10. Following the money: unaccounted AI spend (theme 2) traced to Felix's sign-off, based on output numbers that looked good and nothing else.
11. Codebase drift (theme 3): a technical deep dive — Tariq Farouk introduced, helping Maya see that nobody has a full picture of the system that failed. **Also plant the first small, unexplained crack of the "collective unease" thread (see theme 9 in `themes-to-dramatize.md`)** — a senior engineer says something offhand that echoes Sam's chapter 9 sentence, brief and unremarked-on for now, paying off properly at Josh's confrontation in chapter 18.
12. Review breakdown (theme 4): discovering how review became "kick the tyres," and that Ruth's rigor was read as her "slowing things down."
13. Documentation's double edge (theme 5): the AI-generated docs are found to have actually *contained* the warning — nobody trusted them enough to read them. Direct payoff of the premise's key line.
14. Ungoverned skills (theme 6): tracing the specific skill/prompt pattern that generated the vulnerability, and how it silently degraded over months; light touch on theme 10 (open source) via Tariq's tangled-up library.
15. Midpoint gut-punch: Maya confirms Ruth was right all along and was sidelined for it; brings early findings to Felix and Ashworth as pressure mounts for a name.

### Act 3 — The reckoning (chapters 16–20, ~11,000 words)
16. Boardroom pressure: Ashworth wants a scapegoat (Josh or Sam); Maya pushes back and risks her own contract to do it.
17. Second visit to Shun: Maya makes the "small, empowered team" connection herself before Marcus states it; Aiko's philosophy lands once she reframes it concretely, past Marcus's initial scepticism. **Must also land the sharper judgement-building point (see the note under "Shokunin" in §4a, added 2026-09-11):** not just "AI makes it possible to skip the slow years," but "reading/reviewing fast output doesn't build judgement the way slow, effortful practice does — even done carefully, it isn't a substitute." Maya should connect this explicitly back to Sam in chapter 9 (he read the code properly; it didn't save him) — this is the chapter where that dot gets joined out loud, not before.
18. Maya confronts Josh directly; "I was just doing what got rewarded" — humanises him without excusing the outcome (theme 1/2's human face). **Payoff of the "collective unease" thread seeded in chapter 11 (theme 9):** Maya uses the same interviewing move Marcus used on Sam in chapter 9 — showing she's learned it — and gets underneath Josh's professional composure to something close to Sam's own sentence, just buried longer and defended harder. Not a cheap "he feels it too" beat; the point is the whole function has been running on a shared unhappiness nobody had permission to name until Sam did it by accident.
19. Sam's low point and the beginning of his re-framing: Maya and Marcus validate his grief about the craft rather than dismissing it, alongside clearing him of individual blame (theme 9).
20. Maya presents the structural diagnosis to the board, fights for Ruth's vindication and the small-team redesign over Ashworth's scapegoat instinct; Felix, out of his depth, has to decide who to trust.

### Act 4 — The turn (chapters 21–24, ~7,000 words)
21. The small-team restructure is piloted on one slice of the programme, folding Ruth in as its safety backbone; early friction, then it visibly works. **Must also introduce the "rediscovered joy" beat (Colin, 2026-09-11) — the positive counterweight to Sam's and Josh's grief that the book hasn't earned yet.** Not "AI took the joy out of engineering," but "the joy relocated, and nobody told anyone where it went": engineering hasn't disappeared, it's shifted to architecture, judgement, and taste — all still theirs — and product thinking (what this actually does for the person on the other end, which in this setting is a patient's health, about as concrete as "impact" gets) is its own real source of meaning, separate from the craft of writing lines of code. Should be *shown* happening inside the pilot team, on the page — Sam ideally at the centre of it, since he's carried the grief arc — not asserted as a lesson. Ties directly to Colin's own "Product Thinking Over Delivery Thinking" principle (`the-future-of-software-development.md`), which the book hasn't dramatized yet. **Deliberately not added as a sixth principle to chapter 24's "Five things" document** — that document is scoped as investigation lessons Maya writes for a junior investigator, not organisational/engineering advice, so a joy/product-thinking principle would be a tonal mismatch there. This theme belongs entirely in Act 4's narrative, shown rather than told.
22. Wider rollout agreed; Ashworth claims credit publicly (realistically, not a clean redemption); Felix visibly leaning on Maya and Ruth's judgement now rather than metrics alone.
23. A short, quiet chapter: Tariq's "software for one" side project gets its grace-note moment (theme 11); a final visit to Shun and a single closing line from Aiko.
24. Closing chapter: the distilled framework, stated plainly — Maya and Marcus's hard-won principles, playing the same role as *The Phoenix Project*'s Three Ways/Four Types of Work appendix.

**Open notes for the next pass:** this is scene-list-level, not yet dialogue or prose; several chapters (7, 9, 13, 17) are doing a lot of thematic work in a short space and may need to split into two once drafted. Chapter 24's actual framework content still needs writing (see README's open question on whether Colin's "Future of Software Development" principles should *be* it).
