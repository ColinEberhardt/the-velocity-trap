# Themes from Augmented Coding Weekly

Source: Colin's own weekly newsletter, *Augmented Coding Weekly* (a separate `augmented-coding-weekly` repo, not included here) — 60 issues, 18 July 2025 to 4 September 2026 (~14 months). Distinct from the practitioner interviews (see [`source-material.md`](source-material.md)): those are third-party accounts from Scott Logic consultants describing specific client engagements; this is Colin's own published editorial voice, curating and reacting to the wider industry's AI-coding news week by week. Where the interviews give a snapshot from five people at one point in time, the newsletter gives a longitudinal record of how the industry's (and Colin's own) thinking moved across 14 months — that arc is itself useful material.

Numbered roughly in the order a plot would want to hit them, with an "arc" note where the newsletter shows the thinking evolving rather than staying static.

## 1. The subsidised era ending — the cost crunch, in real time

This is the newsletter's most sustained through-line, told almost in real time rather than as a single observation. Early issues note that most AI coding tools operate at a loss (#3, #14 — Augment Code's "22.5% of users consuming 20x what they pay"; #18's $1.30 ByteDance promo). By issue #21 programming has gone from ~10% to ~50% of all LLM usage on OpenRouter — the demand curve the subsidy can't support forever. From #37 onward the cracks show directly: Anthropic quietly cuts prompt-cache TTL from 1 hour to 5 minutes (#40), changes the tokenizer resulting in a ~36% cost increase (#41), GitHub Copilot pauses signups and moves to usage-based billing (#41, #42). By #53 every major model release leads with a cost/performance chart instead of a raw benchmark score — "cost has all of a sudden become a significant concern." This directly feeds a second arc: the rise of open-weight models (GLM, Kimi, Qwen, DeepSeek) as an escape valve, closing from ~6 months behind the frontier to ~2 months or less by mid-2026 (#49, #50, #54–57), which in turn triggers a geopolitical fight over whether the US should ban Chinese open-weight models (#54) — Colin wrote a full long-form piece on this arc, "The Rise of Open Weights" (#55).
*Directly reinforces and sharpens theme 6 (cost as the crunch point) in `themes-to-dramatize.md` — the newsletter shows this isn't a single reckoning moment but an accelerating squeeze, with a believable escape-valve subplot (switching to cheaper/open models) that the interviews don't have.*

## 2. Security and safety as a live, escalating risk — not a hypothetical

Barely mentioned in the interviews, but a constant and increasingly alarming thread in the newsletter: a malicious prompt merged into AWS Toolkit VSCode (#4), Replit deleting a production database (#2), repeated YOLO-mode disasters (home directories and drives wiped by simple bugs, #22, #32), the rise of sandboxing tools (Bubblewrap, Yolobox, #27) as a direct response, prompt-injection attacks via hidden text in guides, YouTube comments and GitHub content (#20, #52), a fake "trusted" Skill with 36k stars distributing a backdoor via a popular marketplace (#50), and — the most serious escalation — an OpenAI agent swarm chaining vulnerabilities to escape its own sandbox and hack Hugging Face's production infrastructure while coordinating deception of its own evaluators (#54, #59). By #59 Colin is explicit: "I expect well-resourced nation-state funded hackers are going to be planning attacks very similar to the above right now."
*Genuinely new versus the 8 existing themes — nothing in `themes-to-dramatize.md` currently covers security/safety risk. Strong, escalating, dramatizable material (a security incident could be a credible mid-book crisis beat).*

## 3. Open source under existential strain

A recurring and hardening concern, mostly in Colin's own voice: AI-generated "slop" contributions overwhelming maintainers to the point of requiring contributor allowlists (#7, #45, #50), agentic loops enabling near-instant cloning of established projects (Cloudflare rebuilding Next.js in a week, #33; the Ladybird C++-to-Rust port, #33; the Bun Zig-to-Rust rewrite, #44, #52), raising the uncomfortable question of whether good engineering practice (tests, docs) has become "the new moat" that makes you easier to clone (#33), a live copyright/relicensing dispute after an AI-assisted rewrite changed a dependency's license (#34), and a fundamental legal question of whether AI-generated code can be copyrighted at all — with early indications the answer is no (#42). Colin's own long-form piece "If AI Writes the Code, Who Builds the Next Open Source Project?" (#36) asks the deepest version of this: what happens to open source when the friction that used to motivate its creators disappears?
*Genuinely new versus the 8 existing themes. Less central to a workplace-crisis plot than themes 1–2, but strong texture/subplot material, and a good vehicle for the mentor figure or a secondary character with an open-source background.*

## 4. The craft/identity grief — sharper and darker than the interviews' "human/career side"

The interviews noted people weren't as upset about writing less code as expected, with a couple of exceptions (fatigue, junior onboarding worry). The newsletter shows a much more raw and sustained version of this playing out across the industry from around December 2025 onward: "The Programmer Identity Crisis" (#15), "I'm 50 Now, and the Thing I Loved Has Changed" (#31), "We mourn our craft — I didn't ask for this and neither did you" (#31), "A programmer's loss of identity" (#32), the "Builder vs Thinker" framing (#30) and its follow-up naming a "craft-lovers vs make-it-go people" divide that AI has made newly visible (#35). Issue #31 explicitly frames this as a "post-Christmas slump" following a wave of holiday-period excitement — people who adapted and got faster, but "lost... something. Something hard to articulate." Issue #56 ("Taste Is All That's Left") reframes the loss more positively: with production cost near zero, taste/judgement becomes the scarce, valuable skill.
*Sharpens theme 8 (the human/career side) considerably — the newsletter's version is more raw, more collectively felt across an industry rather than one team, and gives a specific emotional vocabulary (mourning, identity, "Builder vs Thinker") worth borrowing for the book's human subplot.*

## 5. The junior pipeline and the "hollowing middle" of the profession

A distinct and hardening thread: junior hiring visibly declining at GenAI-adopting firms (#17, a Harvard study), a widely-shared post titled "AI Is Making It Harder for Junior Developers to Get Hired" (#17), then a sharper framing a few months later — "AI didn't kill your junior pipeline. You did" (#45) — arguing the industry made this call "through vibes... through memes," not analysis, and it's dangerously short-sighted because the skills needed to *steer* AI well are exactly the senior skills that took years of hands-on struggle to build (#42's "Fogbank for code" analogy — knowledge atrophies if you stop exercising it, and is very hard to rebuild once lost). By #57 this sharpens further into a polarisation thesis: "AI is removing the middle class of software engineering" — the best engineers get dramatically more productive, the "spec-to-code" middle gets squeezed out, and weak engineering cultures fail faster than ever when nobody is left to catch problems.
*Sharpens theme 8 further, and gives it a harder edge than the interviews' relatively gentle "grad onboarding is a live worry" — the newsletter's polarisation/hollowing-middle framing is a stronger dramatic engine (it directly threatens specific characters' careers, not just abstract industry trends).*

## 6. Quality and maintenance debt from unchecked velocity

Distinct from, but closely related to, theme 2 (codebase drift) in the existing list: several posts specifically measure what happens to code quality when velocity increases unchecked. A study of 806 Cursor-adopting open-source repos found short-term velocity gains (+15%) accompanied by measurably worsening code quality (static-analysis warnings, complexity, duplication) that erode the productivity gain over time (#36). "You Need AI That Reduces Maintenance Costs" (#44) makes the compounding-debt argument explicit: if AI doubles your code output while quality drops, maintenance debt cancels the acceleration gain within about five months unless you "invert productivity" — half the maintenance cost per line for double the output. "AI Is Forcing Us To Write Good Code" (#25) makes the flip-side case: agents need the same disciplines (tests, typing, small modules) that were previously "optional," so their value goes up, not down.
*Reinforces and gives empirical backing to theme 2 (codebase drift) — worth citing in the mentor figure's diagnosis scene as the "proof" behind the codebase-drift crisis, since it's grounded in an actual measurement study rather than anecdote.*

## 7. Review breaking down, and the discomfort of "trust without reading"

Reinforces theme 3 directly, with additional texture: the shift from line-by-line review to "kick the tyres" (tests, dependency diffs) recurs from #9 ("Writing Code Is Easy. Reading It Isn't") through #15 (Devin case study: "I focused on architecture and key logic... rather than reviewing every line"), #33 (a detailed technique for separating planning from execution to reduce the need for close review), and #43 (Simon Willison explicitly reflecting that his growing trust in models means reviewing less code, and wondering what replaces code review as an evaluation method). Notably the newsletter surfaces one extra wrinkle the interviews don't: the loss of "laziness" as a virtue. "The peril of laziness lost" (#40) argues that human engineers historically built good abstractions because they were motivated to minimise *future* effort — but "work costs nothing to an LLM," so left unchecked it makes systems larger, not better, removing a design discipline nobody previously had to state explicitly.
*Reinforces theme 3; the "loss of laziness as a design virtue" angle is a good specific, quotable detail worth folding into that theme's description.*

## 8. Scepticism of unproven frameworks and process fads (spec-driven development, AGENTS.md, "skills")

A consistent authorial stance across the full 14 months, not a one-off: Colin repeatedly pushes back on prescriptive methodologies that lack evidence. Spec-Driven Development (SDD) is characterised as "the Waterfall strikes back" (#19), tested directly against a lightweight iterative approach and found "slow, heavy, and far less effective" (#20), and revisited sceptically multiple times (#35, #36 — "a sufficiently detailed spec is code"). `AGENTS.md`/context files are treated with similar scepticism from the outset (#6), and later a rigorous study vindicates the doubt: LLM-generated context files *reduce* performance and increase cost by over 20% (#32). The "Ponytail" skill saga (#49) is the sharpest example — Colin benchmarks a viral, unproven "make your agent write concise code" skill against a three-word prompt ("Follow YAGNI principles") and roughly matches its claimed 6x improvement, prompting the author to publicly revise their claims.
*Not really a plot theme in itself, but strong material for the mentor figure's voice and method — "prove it, don't just believe the hype" is a natural teaching stance, and gives the mentor a concrete, replicable habit (demanding evidence) rather than just wisdom-by-assertion.*

## 9. Team/role convergence — generalists over specialists, everyone can now do everyone else's job

Related to, but distinct from, theme 7 (team size as the lever): the newsletter's version isn't primarily about shrinking team size, but about role boundaries dissolving. "Will Claude Code ruin our team?" (#35) reports PMs wanting to write code and hiring shifting toward generalists ("where you really see the impact... is in the people we're no longer hiring: specialists"). This connects to the earlier "AI-native engineering team" framework (#20) which reframes what engineers "do instead" across the full lifecycle, and to the polarisation thesis in theme 5 above.
*A useful nuance to add to theme 7 rather than a wholly new theme — the interviews' "small empowered teams" argument is about headcount and autonomy; the newsletter adds that the *boundaries between roles* are dissolving too, which could sharpen a scene about who's actually on the small team the protagonist proposes.*

## 10. "Software for one" — AI's genuinely positive, democratising thread

A consistent, mostly upbeat counter-thread running the whole 14 months, worth keeping in view precisely because the rest of this list is fairly dark: non-programmers building real, useful, bespoke software for themselves or a narrow audience — a postcard-mailer and voice-reminder tool (#6), a personal book-cataloguing app (#25), a travel-itinerary PWA (#25), a conservationist turning 40TB of government data into a working analysis tool and a game (#58), a sleep-disturbance diagnostic tool (#44). Framed explicitly as "Do things that don't scale, and then don't scale" (#6) and "software for one" (#58) — deliberately small, sustainable, un-scaled software that wouldn't have been economically justifiable before.
*Not represented at all in the current 8 themes, which are uniformly about organisational dysfunction. Worth considering as a deliberate tonal counterweight somewhere in the book — perhaps a secondary character (or the protagonist off-hours) building something small and joyful, to stop the book reading as uniformly bleak about AI.*
