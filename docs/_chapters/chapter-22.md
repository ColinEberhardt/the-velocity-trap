The company-wide email went out on a Tuesday, and Maya read it twice before she decided how she felt about it, which turned out to take longer than reading it had.

*Following a comprehensive internal review, Meridian is proud to announce a new operating model for our engineering organisation — smaller, empowered delivery teams with embedded clinical safety expertise at every stage. This reflects our unwavering commitment to patient safety and our determination to lead the industry in the responsible use of AI in healthcare technology.*

Her name wasn't in it. Neither was Ruth's, nor Tariq's, nor Sam's. It read like something that had always been the plan, arrived at calmly, by people who had never once needed a stranger to sit in a boardroom and threaten to be replaced before anyone would listen to it.

"Does that bother you?" Kalu asked, when she mentioned it.

"Less than I expected it to." She meant it, mostly. "I didn't take this job to have my name on an email. I took it to make the thing in the email true. It's true. I can live with who gets to say it."

Ashworth said something similar, in substance, when she called two days later — a proper call this time, not summoned through Kalu, which Maya noted as its own small shift.

"The board's approved the rollout across all of Digital Services. Eighteen months, phased. I wanted you to hear that from me before you saw the press release, because the press release is going to make it sound like this was always where we were headed, and I know that isn't how it happened."

"I appreciate you saying so, even if you're not going to say it publicly."

"I'm not going to say it publicly," Ashworth agreed, without apology, "because the version where the board pre-empted a crisis is more useful to this organisation right now than the version where an external investigator had to threaten to embarrass eleven directors into doing the right thing. Both versions are true. I'm choosing the one that keeps the rollout funded." A pause, and something that wasn't quite warmth but was recognisably its neighbour. "For what it's worth, David asked me last week whether we'd retained you for future work. I told him I hoped we wouldn't need to. I don't think he took that as the compliment I meant it as."

Maya found she didn't need David's conversion, exactly. She'd stopped expecting it somewhere around the second board meeting, and had settled, instead, for something she found she trusted more: an organisation that no longer needed to believe her, because it had started checking things for itself.

---

She saw the proof of that the following week, in a meeting she hadn't been invited to and heard about secondhand, from Tariq, who told it to her with the specific relish of someone reporting a small miracle.

Felix had been asked, in a steering session, to approve an accelerated timeline for a new feature — the kind of request that eight weeks ago he would have waved through on the strength of a velocity chart and a confident product story.

"He asked Ruth first," Tariq said. "Not finance. Not the roadmap. He looked straight at her and said, 'Is this safe to move that fast,' and when she said she needed two more days to be sure, he told the room the timeline moved, not the review." A small, disbelieving shake of his head. "I've worked here four years. I've never seen him ask that question before he'd already decided the answer."

"That's not redemption," Maya said, half to herself. "That's just someone learning which questions were always the ones that mattered."

"Does it need to be redemption?"

She thought about it, properly, the way she'd learned to over the last month rather than reaching for whatever answer arrived first because it was tidiest. "No," she said finally. "I don't think it does. I think it just needs to hold, the next time nobody's watching to make sure it does."
