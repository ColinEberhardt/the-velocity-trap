Felix Adeyemi met her in a room with a demo screen already warmed up, which told Maya something before he'd said a word.

"Maya — can I call you Maya — thank you for doing this so fast." He shook her hand with both of his, the way people did when they'd decided warmth was a strategy. He was younger than she'd expected from the title, mid-forties, dressed like someone who'd been photographed for a company LinkedIn post that morning and hadn't had time to change. "I want to start by showing you what we actually built here, before you see the worst day of it. Is that alright?"

It wasn't a real question, so she let him have it.

He brought up the MeridianCare Assistant on the big screen — a clean chat interface, calm blue, a small friendly avatar that wasn't quite a person and wasn't quite not one. "Eighteen months ago, if you had a concern at nine at night, your options were 111, A&E, or wait until morning and hope. Now you get an intelligent first response in under ten seconds, and if it's serious, it tells you to call an ambulance faster than a human triage nurse would, because it isn't juggling four other calls at once." He said it with the specific fluency of someone who had said it many times, to many rooms, and had never once had to defend the middle of the sentence rather than the ending.

"How many patients use it a week."

"Forty thousand, and climbing. Patient satisfaction's the highest of any digital service we run. NHS England's been asking us to present it twice this year." He smiled, and it was a real smile, which made it worse, somehow, than if it had been a performance. "This is the thing I'm proudest of at this company. Genuinely."

"So walk me through what happened to it, nine days ago."

Felix blinked. "The Assistant? Nothing happened to the Assistant. It's had zero incidents this quarter, I can show you the uptime—"

"Not the Assistant. The Critical Results Escalation service."

He looked at her the way people look at a word in a language they half-recognise. "The — sorry, remind me what that is."

"It's the thing that's supposed to tell a clinician when a patient's blood result means they might die if nobody calls them. It didn't, nine days ago, for a man with kidney disease. He's in a coronary care unit now."

The smile finished the job of disappearing that it had started somewhere around "the Assistant." "Right. Yes. I — that's not a system I have much visibility into, if I'm honest. It's old, it's backend, it's not something we've ever put in front of a patient or a board." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I'd have to ask Josh. That's really his world, not mine."

"You run the digital function, Felix."

"I run the *strategy* for the digital function." A note of something defensive now, thin and reflexive. "I can't be across every legacy service we've got. Nobody could be. My job is the vision, the product, making sure what we build actually serves patients—" He caught himself, heard it, and had the grace to look briefly uncomfortable about it. "I appreciate how that sounds, given the circumstances."

"It sounds," Maya said, not unkindly, "like there's a system forty thousand people a week trust with their health, and a much older, much less glamorous system that's actually deciding whether anyone calls them back — and you can tell me the uptime figures for one and not the name of the other until I said it out loud."

He didn't have an answer to that, which she noted was, in its own way, an answer.

"Is AI part of your remit more broadly, Felix? Not just the Assistant. Whatever your engineers use to actually build things."

He seemed almost relieved to be asked something he could answer. "Technically, yes — it sits under digital transformation, same as everything else with 'AI' in the sentence. I don't think of it that way day to day, though. It's just how people work now."

"Do you know how they use it? Whether it's the same across the board, or whether some people lean on it a lot more than others."

A small pause, longer than the question should have needed. "I'd assume fairly consistently. We haven't mandated anything specific — I didn't want to be prescriptive about how people work, that felt like the wrong instinct for this kind of tool. I genuinely couldn't tell you the detail of who's using what, or how much."

"Who reviews what goes into a system like Escalation before it ships?"

"The engineering team. Josh runs a strong team, very autonomous, I don't like to micromanage how they work — that's not what I'm here for." He said this as though reciting a job description he genuinely believed in, which, she was starting to suspect, he did.

"That's not what I asked. I asked who reads the code. Not who owns the roadmap."

"The team reviews each other's work, obviously. That's standard practice." A small note of something — not defensiveness exactly, more the particular unease of a man being asked a question in a language he technically speaks but has never had to use under pressure. "I could get you the exact process from Josh. I'm not — that level of detail isn't really my—" He stopped himself, and to his credit, didn't finish the sentence with a lie. "I trust my engineering leads to run engineering. I trust my product sense to know when the story we're telling is true. Those have always been the right things for me to trust."

*Have they,* Maya thought, and didn't say it, because it was Tuesday and she had eleven days and there would be a much better moment to ask him that question than this one.

"One more thing," she said instead. "You said you couldn't tell me who's using AI tools how much. Do you know what it's costing you? Across engineering, not the Assistant."

Felix looked, for the first time since she'd walked in, genuinely uncertain. "I'd have to check with finance. It's — it's not a number I carry around, I'll be honest. We give teams the tools they need and trust them to use them well. Micromanaging spend on something like this felt like exactly the wrong signal to send, when what we wanted was for people to feel empowered—"

"Empowered," Maya repeated, not unkindly, and wrote the word down, because she had learned a long time ago that the words people reached for to describe the thing they hadn't looked at closely were almost always the most useful ones in the file.

Felix walked her to Josh Whitfield's floor himself, still talking — about the roadmap, about NHS England, about a demo he'd given a minister eight months ago that had gone extremely well — and Maya let him talk, because a man who talked this freely about what he was proud of would, eventually, without meaning to, tell her exactly where he'd stopped looking.
