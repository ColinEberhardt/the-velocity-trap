Four months after Meridian, Maya sat at her own desk — not a client's, her own, the one she paid rent on — and opened a blank document instead of a template.

That was the first thing she noticed about herself doing it. She always started from a template. The template had headings that did half the thinking for you: *Executive Summary. Root Cause. Recommendations.* Headings that made a report feel finished before you'd earned it.

This one she wanted to write clean, the way you'd write something you actually meant.

It wasn't for Meridian. Meridian's official report had gone out weeks ago, signed off by Deborah Ashworth's communications team, thirty-one pages, appropriately grave, appropriately vague in the two places that mattered least and appropriately precise in the two that mattered most. Maya had fought for those two. It had been enough.

This one was for Priya.

Priya was twenty-six, sharp, three years into the firm, and in eleven days she was flying out alone to lead her first investigation — a mid-sized logistics company, an AI-authored routing change that had put a truck through a level crossing barrier forty seconds before conflicting text arrived from the depot. Nobody hurt. This time. Maya had read the file twice and recognised the shape of it the way you recognise a face in bad light: not the details, the *silhouette*.

She could have taken Priya for a coffee and talked her through it. She'd done that for the last two juniors who'd gone out solo, and it had gone fine, and she'd felt good about it, and neither of them would have been able to repeat back to her a single thing she'd said a week later, because talk is generous with itself. Talk lets you sound wise without committing to anything a person could hold you to later.

Eight years ago she had written a clean report about a different plant, a different near-miss, and a name where a systemic cause should have gone, and it had been generous with itself in exactly that way. She had never told anyone what happened eighteen months after that report shipped. She still hadn't, not even Marcus, not in so many words — though the week after Meridian closed out, over a meal at Shun that neither of them had needed to explain to the other, she'd got as far as *there's an old case I think about more than I let on,* and Marcus had looked at her for a while and said, "I know," and ordered them both another round of the yuzu, and that had somehow been enough, for now.

So this time she wrote it down. Not because Priya couldn't be trusted with a conversation, but because Maya couldn't be trusted with one — not on this particular subject. Writing made her show her working. It always had. That was exactly why she'd spent a career using it to hide the working instead.

She titled it plainly: *Five things I wish someone had made me write down before my first one.*

---

**1. If nobody can see both ends, nobody's actually accountable.**

At Meridian, the engineer who'd shipped the pattern and the clinician whose patients depended on it worked in the same organisation and had never once been in a room together. Nobody had engineered that distance on purpose. It had simply been allowed to grow, the way distance always grows when nobody's job is to close it. Small, empowered teams aren't a productivity idea. They're the minimum unit of organisation in which a person can actually see the consequence of their own decision — which turns out to be the minimum unit in which "accountability" means anything at all, rather than being a word on a slide.

*You will be tempted to treat this as an org-chart problem. It is not an org-chart problem. It is a distance problem, and org charts are only one of the ways distance gets made.*

**2. Don't trust an artefact for being well-written.**

The thing that undid me at Meridian wasn't a lie. Nobody lied. It was a report — plans, documentation, a tidy set of decision records — that read as competent because it was fluent, and fluency isn't evidence. Felix Adeyemi wasn't stupid and he wasn't being deceived on purpose; he was reading exactly the artefacts that had been built to be read, by systems that are very good at sounding right and only sometimes are. Marcus has one question that covers this entire discipline and I am going to make you memorise it before you fly out: *how do you know that?* Not "does it sound plausible." Not "is it well formatted." *How do you know.*

**3. The system failed. Start there, not with a name.**

Every investigation will hand you a name early, usually within the first two days, usually offered up freely by someone who is not that name. Write it down. Then put it in a drawer and don't open the drawer again until you understand *why* that person did the thing they did — what they were rewarded for, what they were never taught, what the loudest incentive in the room was telling them, quietly, every single day. Josh Whitfield did what Meridian was paying him, structurally, to do. That is not an excuse. It is the actual finding, and the actual finding is the only one that prevents the next one.

**4. Protect the years that used to be boring.**

There is a version of expertise that only gets built by getting stuck — really stuck, for long enough that you have to work it out yourself, or fail trying. AI has made it possible to route around that entirely, for a junior, without anyone noticing it's happened, because the output still looks fine. It didn't look fine. It looked *fine* right up until it mattered, and the person closest to it had never once, in his whole career, hit the wall that would have taught him to ask the question that would have saved us all four very bad weeks. Somebody has to keep deliberately putting juniors in front of that wall. It will feel, every single time, like slowing them down. It is the opposite of that.

**5. Attention is the one thing you can't speed up.**

Everything about how fast software gets made has changed in the time it took me to write this sentence. The thing that hasn't changed, and I don't think ever will, is that understanding a decision — really understanding it, the way you'd want understood the decision that affects your own care, if you were the patient — takes a fixed, non-negotiable amount of human attention, and no model release changes that number. Aiko has a phrase for this I've started stealing without asking her: *ichigo ichie* — one time, one meeting. Every decision your team makes is, in the way that actually matters, happening for the first and only time. Read it like that's true, because it is.

---

She read it back once, changed "start with a name" to "start there, not with a name" because it read better and she genuinely believed it more that way round, and sent it before she could template it into something safer.

Marcus texted forty minutes later. *Only five?*

*Five's what I've got,* she wrote back. *Ask me again in ten years.*

She sat with that for a second — the idea that there'd be a version of her, ten years from now, with a longer list and a steadier hand — and found, for the first time in longer than she could easily place, that she was looking forward to becoming her.
