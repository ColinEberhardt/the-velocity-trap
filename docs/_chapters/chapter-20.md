Maya brought Ruth Okafor into the boardroom with her, which she hadn't asked anyone's permission to do, because she'd learned in the last four days exactly how much permission was worth asking for versus simply taking.

David clocked it immediately. "This is a board session, Ms Reyes, not—"

"Ruth Okafor identified the exact failure mode that put a man in a coronary care unit, in writing, with a working reproduction, eleven months before it happened. If this board is going to hear a diagnosis, it's going to hear it from the person who made it first, not just the person who was paid to rediscover it afterward." She didn't sit. "I'll give you the summary. Ruth can answer anything technical better than I can, and I'd rather you heard the hard parts from someone who has no reason to soften them for you."

Nobody objected again.

"There is no single person responsible for what happened to that patient," Maya said. "There's a shared safety instruction that was quietly stripped of its own caution over fourteen months by six people, none of whom benchmarked whether the change was safe. There's a review process that takes four minutes because nobody's been given longer, reviewing changes made by a junior engineer who read every line carefully and still didn't have the judgement to know what he was missing, because that judgement takes years nobody protected for him. There's a warning that survived being written clearly and died being summarised for a slide. There's a budget that grew because it was measured against speed and never against understanding. And there's an engineer" — she gestured, once, toward Ruth — "who did everything right, on the record, for eleven months, and was told her caution was an obstacle to pace."

"That's a great deal of blame distributed very thinly," David said. "Boards prefer decisions to arithmetic, Ms Reyes."

"I'm not here to distribute blame. I'm here to tell you what to change so this doesn't happen again in eighteen months to a different patient, in a different system, because I promise you the pattern is sitting in more than one place in this building." She let that land. "Break the large teams up. Small, empowered, accountable groups who can see the whole shape of what they own, the way nobody currently can. Make review time a resourced part of the job, not something squeezed into four minutes because nothing else was left. Put Ruth in charge of a formal safety-review function with real authority to block a change, not just flag it. Test every shared skill and prompt template the way you'd test code, and treat an unverified edit to one as seriously as an unverified edit to a production system, because that's what it is."

"And I'd like to do all four of those on one team first. Not across Digital Services. One team." She let that sit too, because she suspected it would land as timidity to at least half the room, and wanted them to sit with that assumption before she took it away from them. "I've spent the last week learning something from a woman who runs a kitchen on exactly one principle: the sweeping fix is usually the wrong one, because you can't verify a change at that size until it's already too late to undo cheaply. Small, watched properly, then trusted — never big, announced, and hoped. If I hand you a plan to change everything at once, I'm asking you to trust me the same way Felix trusted a dashboard he didn't know how to question. I'd rather earn it, on one team, before it becomes everyone's problem to solve the same way."

"I want to be honest about two things that aren't on that list," she went on, before anyone could treat it as finished. "I don't know how you make AI-written documentation say what actually matters instead of saying everything at the same volume. Nobody's solved that properly yet, anywhere — I've read what's been tried. And I don't know how you make AI spend visible enough to govern the way you'd govern headcount, when the whole industry hasn't worked that out either. I'm not going to stand here and hand you a tidy fifth and sixth point that pretend those are solved, because they aren't, and I'd rather you knew that today than find out in eighteen months that I'd smoothed it over to make this meeting land better."

"Then what, precisely, are you asking us for," David said, "if not a fix?"

"Permission to keep asking the question properly, in public, with someone accountable for the asking. That's most of what's been missing — not the answer, the ownership of not having one yet." Maya glanced, briefly, at Ruth. "Put both problems formally inside the safety-review function. Not as an afterthought bolted onto it later — as part of the job from day one."

It was Ashworth who answered, not David, in the tone of someone deciding something rather than performing a decision for the room. "Then that's what the budget's for," she said. "I'd rather fund two problems we're honest about not having solved than one we pretend we have. Ruth — bring me a proper case next quarter. Take whatever time it needs to be right, not fast."

Felix, who had said nothing since Maya started, finally spoke — and Maya watched him decide, visibly, in real time, which version of himself was going to answer.

"I want to say something, and I want it on the record." His voice wasn't steady, quite, but it held. "I signed off on spend I didn't understand, based on numbers I didn't know how to question, because questioning them wasn't a skill anyone had ever asked me to have. I trusted the wrong things, for defensible reasons, and a man nearly died because of it, alongside a great many other people's decisions that were also defensible in isolation. I don't think firing me fixes that any more than firing Sam would have. I think what fixes it is doing exactly what Maya's describing, and I'd like to be the person who makes sure it actually happens, if the board will let me be, because I don't think I'm the right person to design it — but I know now, at least, that I need someone who is."

It wasn't redemption. Maya didn't think it was meant to be. It was, she thought, watching Ashworth's face do something complicated and unreadable, the first true sentence Felix had said since she'd arrived that wasn't in service of protecting himself, and it was worth more for that than for anything it actually fixed on its own.

"Ruth," Ashworth said, addressing her directly for the first time in the meeting. "If the board approves this. Would you take that role."

Ruth's answer, when it came, was as measured and unhurried as everything else about her. "I've been trying to take that role for eleven months. I'd take it formally, yes. I'd also want it in writing that the authority means something, this time, rather than existing on paper the way the last version of caution did."

"Agreed," Ashworth said, before David could speak again, and something in the finality of it told Maya the decision had actually been made somewhere before this meeting started, quietly, in a conversation she hadn't been in the room for.

David didn't look pleased. He didn't look like a man who'd lost, either — more like a man recalculating, already, what winning would look like from here. But he didn't push again, and for the first time in four days, Maya let herself believe that might be enough.
