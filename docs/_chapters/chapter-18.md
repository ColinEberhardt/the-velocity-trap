Josh had cleared his calendar without being asked, which told Maya something before she'd said a word — a man arranging his own defence in advance, the way you clear a room before you expect to be shouted at in it.

"Before you start," he said, "I know how this looks. Declining Ruth's review request. The budget sign-off. I've had four days to read my own name in your interim notes and I know exactly which five decisions you're going to put in front of me."

"I'm not here for the five decisions."

That stopped him more than she'd expected. "Then what."

"I want to know what it's actually like. Running this team, this year. Not the version you'd give a board." She sat, unhurried, the way Marcus sat, the way she'd watched him do it in a room down the corridor, days ago now, without understanding yet that she was being taught something. "I found your velocity dashboard. I know what it says. I want to know what you actually think when you look at it, alone, at the end of a day."

Josh's jaw did the small, controlled thing it did. "I think it's good work. I think this team ships more, faster, with fewer people, than any team I've run in fifteen years. I think that's worth being proud of."

"I believe that you think that. I don't believe it's the whole of what you think."

"You're not my therapist, Ms Reyes."

"No. I'm the person who's spent nine days finding out that Sam Ilyas — twenty-three years old, four months in — was carrying something close to grief about what this job's become, and telling nobody, because he assumed everyone around him had already made their peace with it and he'd look foolish saying otherwise. I'd like to know if that assumption was correct." She let the silence hold. "Was it?"

Something in Josh's face moved that he clearly hadn't given permission to move.

"I was just doing what got rewarded," he said, which she recognised immediately as the true sentence arriving disguised as a deflection, the way people's most honest lines often did. "Velocity's the number everyone above me asks for. It's the number that got me this role. It's the number I can point to when someone asks whether the investment in these tools is paying off. I didn't invent that scoreboard. I just play to it, because it's the only one anyone's ever handed me."

"That's the defence. I asked what you actually think."

He was quiet long enough that Maya wondered if he'd simply refuse, and made herself wait it out the way Marcus would have, saying nothing, letting the silence do the asking instead of her.

"I think," Josh said, finally, slower, "that I used to know exactly what my team had built, line by line, because I'd built half of it myself and reviewed the rest properly. I could have told you, two years ago, why any piece of it worked the way it did. I can't tell you that now. Nobody on my team can, about most of what we ship — not because we're careless, because there's simply more of it than any of us could hold in our heads at the speed we're required to hold it. I noticed that a while ago. I didn't say anything, because saying it out loud sounds like admitting the thing that made me look good for eighteen months was never actually safe, and I've got a mortgage and two kids and a board that's never once asked me how well I understand what we ship, only how fast we ship it." He looked at her properly, for the first time in the conversation, something raw showing through the composure he'd arrived with. "Is that what you wanted?"

"That's exactly what Sam said to me, in slightly different words, on my first day in this building, and he thought he was the only person in it feeling it."

Josh didn't have an answer to that. Maya watched him sit with it — the specific, quiet devastation of a senior man realising the thing he'd assumed was a junior's inexperience was, underneath the years and the mortgage and the professional composure, the exact same thing he'd been carrying himself, unspoken, for longer than he could easily admit.

"I'm not going to put that sentence in my report," Maya said, and meant it. "It's not evidence of anything except that this whole function has been running on a shared unhappiness nobody had permission to name, and I don't think naming it in a report to the board helps anyone. But I needed to hear it from you directly, because the fix I'm about to recommend only works if the people running it actually believe the problem was never about who tried hardest. It was about what everyone was ever allowed to say out loud."
