Finance took two days to produce a number Felix had told her, in front of a demo screen, that he didn't carry around. Maya suspected it had taken two days because nobody had ever asked for it in this shape before, and building the shape had been harder than anyone wanted to admit.

She spread it across the table for Marcus anyway, because he insisted on paper even now, in a world that had mostly stopped printing anything.

Per-engineer monthly spend on AI coding tools, Digital Services, last full quarter. Ninety-one names, anonymised to employee numbers for the version finance had been willing to release without a second sign-off. The number at the top of the list — £4,180 a month, one individual, one licence, running what the notes described as "parallel agent sessions" — sat two hundred and eighty times higher than the number near the bottom, which belonged to somebody spending £15 a month on what looked, from the pattern, like autocomplete and nothing more.

"There's your usage divide," Marcus said, unsurprised, tracing a finger down the column without touching the paper. "Nobody built this. Nobody chose it, in the sense of sitting in a room and deciding it should look like this. It's just what happens when you hand people a tool, say 'use it well,' and don't watch what 'well' comes to mean for ninety-one different definitions of the word."

"Employee 0043." Maya turned the page. "That's the top spender. I can get Kalu to de-anonymise it in about ten minutes if it matters."

"It'll matter eventually. It doesn't matter yet." Marcus tapped the bottom of the list instead. "This one interests me more. Fifteen pounds a month, for six months running, dead flat, no variation. That's not someone who forgot the tool exists. That's someone making a decision, every month, not to use it, and sticking to it hard enough that it shows up as a straight line on a graph."

Maya didn't need finance to tell her who that was. She'd read the personnel file three days ago. "Ruth."

"I'd put money on it before I'd even seen the number." Something that wasn't quite a smile. "Consistency like that isn't accidental. It's a position, held under pressure, for six months, without anyone noticing it was a position at all — because nobody was looking at spend as a signal of anything except cost."

Maya pulled the second document she'd found, the one that had actually kept her up past midnight: an approval thread, four months old, Felix's name on the sign-off line, requesting a forty per cent increase to the AI tooling budget for Digital Services.

*Justification: velocity data (attached) shows 60% increase in deployment frequency since AI tooling rollout began. Recommend expanding licences to full team and removing per-seat spend caps to sustain momentum through Platform Modernisation delivery.*

Attached: the dashboard. The same green bars, the same climbing line, that Felix had shown her, proudly, on her first morning.

"No risk section," Maya said. "No mention of review coverage, test coverage, nothing about what the increase in output was actually made of. Just — it's fast, so let's make it faster."

"How do you know it wasn't discussed and just not written down?"

She checked. Twice. "Because I asked Kalu this morning whether there'd been a risk assessment attached to any AI tooling budget decision in the last eighteen months, and he said, and I quote, 'I don't believe we've ever been asked to produce one, so I don't know how I'd tell you.'"

"That," Marcus said, "is a more honest answer than most people manage to give by accident." He sat back, and for a moment looked less like an investigator and more like a man doing arithmetic he found genuinely sad rather than merely useful. "So here's your shape, so far. Money follows velocity. Velocity gets measured because it's the easiest thing in the building to measure. Nobody measures the thing that actually matters, which is whether the team understands what it's shipping, because there isn't a dashboard for that, and you can't put a number nobody's built on a slide. Felix isn't lying when he says the team's been extraordinary. He's reading the only instrument he's ever been given, and the instrument was never plugged into the thing that broke."

"That's not a defence of him."

"It isn't meant to be one. It's a diagnosis. There's a difference, and the difference matters enormously for what you write in your report, because one of those words gets him fired and the other one gets the actual problem fixed, and I'd hate for you to reach for the wrong one just because it's more satisfying to write."

Maya looked at the two hundred and eighty-to-one gap on the page in front of her, and thought about Ruth's flat, deliberate £15 a month, and Sam's quiet evening code that nobody at Meridian would ever see, and Josh's dashboard, green all the way across, measuring everything except the two people in this building who'd actually understood what was happening.

"We're going to need to talk to Josh's whole team," she said. "Not just Sam."

"Yes," Marcus agreed. "But not yet. There's a codebase I want to look at first, and I have a suspicion neither of us is going to enjoy what it looks like once somebody actually draws a map of it."
