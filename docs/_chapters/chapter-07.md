Marcus Kwan arrived at Meridian carrying a single canvas bag and no laptop, which Maya had forgotten to expect, even though she'd seen him work exactly this way for six years.

"You'll want to write things down," she said, by way of hello.

"I'll want *you* to write things down." He looked, if anything, smaller than she remembered — late sixties, grey suit that had never once been fashionable and would therefore never go out of it, the specific stillness of a man who had decided a long time ago that most rooms calmed down if he did. "I remember things. Writing them down is how people convince themselves they've understood something before they have."

She took him to the room Kalu had given her — glass walls, a view of nothing, a whiteboard someone had wiped so aggressively it still had ghosts on it — and talked for eleven minutes straight. The incident. The timeline. Sam. Ruth's document. Felix and his uptime figures. Josh and his velocity dashboard he hadn't actually shown her but that she'd heard about from three separate people now, unprompted, like a scripture. She finished with the thing she was proudest of finding, because she wanted to see his face when she said it.

"So the root cause is the refactor. Sam's PR. Six weeks ago."

Marcus didn't write anything down. "How do you know that?"

"Because that's what changed the code path that failed."

"That's what changed the code path *most recently*. Those aren't the same sentence, even if they sound like they are from where you're standing." He finally sat, folding himself into the chair like a man arranging furniture he didn't fully trust. "Ruth wrote her warning eleven months ago. The code she was warning about is older than that — old enough that nobody currently at the company can tell you why it's shaped the way it is. So which is the root cause. The six-week-old change, or the four-year-old decision to leave a piece of undocumented, unowned, business-critical logic sitting there long enough that eventually someone was always going to touch it without understanding it?"

Maya opened her mouth, then closed it, because she recognised the shape of the trap and had no honest way out of it.

"Both," she said eventually.

"Both is a real answer. Both is also the answer people give when they haven't decided which one actually matters for what they're about to recommend." He looked at her properly for the first time since he'd sat down — not unkindly, but with the specific attention of a man checking a reading on an instrument. "You wrote the six-week version down first, in your head, before you'd even finished your own sentence. Why."

"Because it's satisfying. It's got a name and a date and a PR number." She said it before she'd decided to, which was, she reflected, exactly the effect he'd always had on her. "It's easier."

"It's easier," Marcus agreed. "That's not the same as true. It might turn out to *be* true. But you don't get to know that yet, and neither do I, and the only way either of us finds out is by being suspicious of every sentence in this room that felt satisfying to say out loud — starting with mine, when I get to that point, which I will, because I'm not immune to it either. I've just had longer to notice."

"So where do we start."

"Not with the code." He stood again, already restless, already halfway to a decision she hadn't seen him make. "I'm hungry, and you look like you haven't eaten anything that wasn't handed to you through a car window in three days. There's somewhere I want to take you."

"Marcus. We have eleven days. Nine, now."

"Then we have time for dinner," he said, "because I am not going to be useful to you until I've had one proper meal and you've stopped vibrating slightly, and both of those problems have the same solution."

She wanted to argue. She found, examining the impulse, that she didn't actually have one — only the reflex of a woman who had spent eight years believing that stopping was the same as failing, and she let him walk her out of the building instead of saying any of that, because some things were easier to disprove by doing the opposite of them than by explaining why they were wrong.
